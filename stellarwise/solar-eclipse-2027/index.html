Solar eclipse of 2 August 2027

On 2 August 2027 the Moon covers the whole Sun along a narrow path from southern Spain and northern Morocco across North Africa to Saudi Arabia and Yemen. Near Luxor, in Egypt, totality lasts more than 6 minutes. Europe, North and East Africa and the Middle East see a partial eclipse. Choose your city for the times.

    Eye safety. Never look at the Sun without proper eclipse glasses, and never point binoculars, a camera or a telescope at it without a proper solar filter on the front.

    The path of totality

    The Moon’s full shadow first touches the Atlantic, then crosses the Strait of Gibraltar, northern Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya and Egypt, and the Red Sea to Saudi Arabia and Yemen, before leaving over the Indian Ocean. Of the cities in the table, Cádiz, Málaga, Gibraltar, Tangier, Benghazi, Luxor, Jeddah, Mecca and Tripoli are inside the path. Greatest eclipse is at 10:06 UTC, near 25.5°N, 33.2°E, close to Luxor.

    Just outside the path, a partial eclipse can come very close to total and still never get dark: the last sliver of Sun is far too bright to look at. Only inside the path does the sky go dark and the Sun’s corona appear.

    Watching it safely

    Look at a partial eclipse only through eclipse glasses or a solar viewer made to the ISO 12312-2 standard, and put a proper solar filter on the front of binoculars, cameras and telescopes. Inside the path, you can take the glasses off only during totality, when the Sun is completely covered, and must put them back on as soon as it starts to reappear. Read solar eclipse safety before the day.

    How the times are worked out

    The tool and the table use the same astronomy code as the Stellarwise app. Our greatest eclipse, 10:06 UTC at 25.5°N 33.2°E, agrees with EclipseWise’s published figures to within a few seconds and a few kilometres. Times are for sea level; the edges of the path, and exact contact times, can shift by a few seconds with your height and the Moon’s uneven edge.

    Durations of totality are rounded to 5 seconds. They use the Moon’s mean radius, so they run a few seconds longer than tables that use the smaller radius of the Moon’s valleys: about 6 min 25 s at greatest eclipse here, against EclipseWise’s 6 min 23 s.
