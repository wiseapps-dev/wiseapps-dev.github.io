How to tell a planet from a star

Planets usually shine with a steady light while stars twinkle, and planets always appear near the ecliptic, the path the Sun takes across the sky. The very brightest point of light after dark, other than the Moon, is almost always Venus or Jupiter. Over a week or two, a planet moves against the stars.

Updated 22 November 2026

Five planets are easy to see without a telescope: Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn. On any night, some of them are up. Here is how to pick them out from the thousands of stars.

1. Does it twinkle?

Stars are so far away that they are points of light, and the moving layers of air above you bend that light this way and that. The star seems to flicker and even flash colours. Planets are close enough to show a tiny disc, and the flickering from different parts of the disc evens out, so a planet shines with a calmer, steadier light.

The test works best high in the sky. Close to the horizon you look through far more air, and even a planet can twinkle there.

2. Is it near the Sun’s path?

The planets go round the Sun in nearly the same flat plane as the Earth, so from here they always appear close to the ecliptic, the line the Sun follows across the sky during the year. The Moon stays near it too. If a bright dot sits roughly along the arc the Sun took that day, or near the Moon, it may well be a planet. A bright point far from that arc, near the pole for example, is a star.

3. Is it brighter than everything else?

Brightness is measured in magnitudes, where smaller numbers are brighter. The brightest star in the night sky, Sirius, is about magnitude −1.5.

Object Typical brightness What it looks like
Venus about −4 Dazzling white, only in the evening or morning twilight
Jupiter about −2 to −2.9 Steady cream-white, brighter than any star
Mars from −2.9 to +1.8 Orange-red; brightest when it is closest to us
Saturn about 0 to +1 Pale yellow, like a fairly bright star
Mercury about −1 to +1 Low in bright twilight, never far from the Sun

So if you see something brighter than Sirius that isn’t the Moon, it is a planet.

4. What colour is it?

Mars has an orange-red tint, and Saturn is a soft yellow. Colour alone can mislead, though: the stars Betelgeuse in Orion and Antares in Scorpius are red too. Antares means “rival of Mars”, because the two can look alike when they are near each other.

5. Does it move?

Watch the same patch of sky over a week or two. Stars keep their places in the constellations; planets drift slowly against them. The word planet comes from the Greek for wanderer. Mercury, Venus and Mars move fastest; Saturn takes about two and a half years to cross one constellation of the zodiac.

Binoculars settle it

Through ordinary binoculars held steady, a star stays a point while a planet shows a small disc. Jupiter is the best test: you can often see up to four of its moons in a line beside it, changing position from night to night.
