Why your phone’s compass is off

A phone’s compass is a magnetometer, a sensor that feels the Earth’s weak magnetic field. Nearby metal, magnets and electronics easily disturb it, and magnetic north is not true north. Move the phone in a figure 8 to calibrate it, step away from metal, and correct for magnetic declination to point at true north.

Updated 22 November 2026

Star apps lean on the compass to know which way your phone is pointing. When the names on the screen don’t line up with the sky, the compass is nearly always the reason.

What the phone actually measures

Your phone has a magnetometer: a sensor that measures the strength and direction of the magnetic field around it. The Earth’s field is weak, so anything magnetic nearby adds to it and drags the reading off:

  • steel railings, car bodies, metal tables and reinforced concrete;
  • magnets in phone cases, wallets, car mounts and speakers;
  • the phone’s own electronics, especially while charging.

Google’s Maps team named charging and walking near a metal pole as typical causes of a wide, unsure heading beam.

Calibrate with a figure 8

Phones correct for their own magnetic quirks by measuring the field in many directions. Hold the phone and move it in a figure 8 a few times, turning your wrist as you go. Google suggests the same for Maps, and Stellarwise asks for it when the compass is unsure.

Then move away from metal. A few steps from a car or a balcony railing often makes more difference than any calibration.

Magnetic north is not true north

A compass points to magnetic north, not to the North Pole. The angle between the two, the magnetic declination, depends on where you are and slowly changes year by year. In 2026 it is roughly 13 degrees west in New York, about 1 degree east in London and about 13 degrees east in Sydney: enough to put the wrong star in the middle of the screen.

The World Magnetic Model, produced by NOAA’s National Centers for Environmental Information and the British Geological Survey and updated at least every five years, gives the declination anywhere on Earth. NOAA notes that smartphone and consumer electronics companies rely on it for compass apps and maps. The current version is WMM2025.

How accurate can a phone compass be?

Even calibrated and away from metal, a phone compass is often a few degrees off. Expect it to be right to within about 5 degrees at best, which is the width of three fingers held at arm’s length, and much worse near metal. That is fine for finding Jupiter, the Moon or a constellation, and not enough to centre a faint object in a telescope.
