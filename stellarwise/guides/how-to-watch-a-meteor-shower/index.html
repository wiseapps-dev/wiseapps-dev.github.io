How to watch a meteor shower

Go somewhere dark, let your eyes adjust for at least 20 minutes, lie back and look at a wide patch of sky about halfway up, away from the radiant. The best hours are usually after midnight, when the radiant is high. You need no telescope or binoculars, just patience and warm clothes.

Updated 22 November 2026

A meteor shower happens when the Earth passes through a trail of dust left by a comet or an asteroid. The grains, most smaller than a grain of sand, burn up in the upper atmosphere as streaks of light. Because they all travel in parallel, their paths seem to spread out from one point in the sky, the radiant, which gives the shower its name: the Perseids from Perseus, the Geminids from Gemini.

When to go out

  • On the peak night, or the night either side. Most showers are worth watching for a few nights around the peak. The Quadrantids are the exception, with a peak that lasts only a few hours.
  • When the radiant is high. For most showers that means after midnight and before dawn. The Geminids are an exception: Gemini is up from mid-evening, so they are good from around 10 pm.
  • When the Moon is down or thin. A bright Moon hides the fainter meteors, which are most of them. Check the Moon column of the meteor shower calendar.

Why you see fewer than the headline number

Shower forecasts quote a ZHR, or zenithal hourly rate: the number one observer would see in an hour under a perfectly dark sky with the radiant straight overhead. Real counts are lower. The International Meteor Organization gives these figures for a shower with a ZHR of 100:

Radiant height Meteors an hour
70° about 94
30° about 50
10° about 17

That is the sine of the radiant’s height times the ZHR, and it is the rule Stellarwise uses for its rate from your place. Light pollution and the Moon cut the numbers further.

Where to look

Don’t stare at the radiant: meteors near it have short trails. The International Meteor Organization suggests a patch of sky 20 to 40 degrees away from it, centred 50 to 70 degrees above the horizon. In practice, lie back and look at whichever part of the sky is darkest and clearest.

Let your eyes adjust

Your eyes need at least 20 minutes in the dark to reach full sensitivity, and one look at a white screen or a torch undoes much of it. Use a red light, and turn on red night mode in Stellarwise before you go out.

What to bring

  • A deck chair, camp bed or mat, so you can look up for an hour without a stiff neck.
  • A sleeping bag or blanket. Even summer nights get cold when you lie still.
  • Patience. Meteors come in bursts and lulls; give it at least half an hour.

You need no binoculars or telescope: their field of view is far too small. Your eyes, with the whole sky in view, are the best instrument.

The big showers

Shower Peak Best from Peak ZHR

Eta Aquariids around 6 May Southern Hemisphere and tropics 50
Perseids 12–13 August Northern Hemisphere 100
