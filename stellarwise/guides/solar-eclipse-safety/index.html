How to watch a solar eclipse safely

Only look at a partial or annular solar eclipse through eclipse glasses or a solar viewer that meets the ISO 12312-2 standard. Ordinary sunglasses are not safe, even very dark ones. Never use binoculars, a camera or a telescope without a solar filter on the front. Only during totality is it safe to look without protection.

Updated 22 November 2026

Eye safety. Never look at the Sun without proper eclipse glasses, and never point binoculars, a camera or a telescope at it without a proper solar filter on the front.

A solar eclipse happens when the Moon passes between the Sun and the Earth. Most people see a partial eclipse, with a bite taken out of the Sun. Inside a narrow path, the Moon covers the Sun completely for a few minutes: a total eclipse. In an annular eclipse the Moon is a little too far away to cover the Sun, and leaves a bright ring.

Use proper eclipse glasses

Looking at the Sun, even when most of it is covered, can burn the retina without any pain, and the damage can be permanent. The American Astronomical Society advises:

  • Use eclipse glasses or a handheld solar viewer that meets the ISO 12312-2 international standard, from a supplier you trust.
  • Check them before use. Throw them away if the filter is scratched, punctured or coming away from the frame.
  • Ordinary sunglasses are not safe, however dark. Nor are smoked glass, photographic film or most welding filters.
  • Put the glasses on before you look up, and look away before you take them off.

Never through binoculars, a camera or a telescope without a front filter

Binoculars, telescopes and camera lenses concentrate sunlight. Looking through them with eclipse glasses on is dangerous too: the concentrated light can burn through the glasses and into your eye. Optics need their own solar filter, fixed securely over the front of the lens. Ask an experienced observer before you try it. A phone camera pointed at the Sun can be damaged, and you should never look at the Sun on the phone screen as a way round the glasses.

Totality is the only exception

During the minutes of totality, when the Moon covers the Sun completely and the corona appears, it is safe to look without glasses. This applies only inside the path of totality and only while the Sun is completely covered. Put the glasses back on as soon as the first bright bead of sunlight reappears. In a partial or annular eclipse there is no safe moment without protection.

No glasses? Use a pinhole

You can watch the partial phases indirectly. Stand with your back to the Sun and let sunlight pass through a small hole onto paper or the ground: a hole poked in card, the holes of a colander, a straw hat, or the gaps between your fingers. Each hole projects a small image of the crescent Sun. Look at the projected image, never through the hole at the Sun.
