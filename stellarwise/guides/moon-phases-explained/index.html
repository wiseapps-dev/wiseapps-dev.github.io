Moon phases explained

The Sun always lights half of the Moon. As the Moon orbits the Earth, about once every 29.5 days, we see more or less of that lit half, which gives the phases from new to full and back. The lit side always faces the Sun, which is why the Moon tilts differently around the world.

Updated 22 November 2026

Half lit, all the time

The Moon makes no light of its own. Like the Earth, it always has a day side lit by the Sun and a night side in darkness. What changes through the month is how much of the day side faces us.

  • At new Moon the Moon is roughly between the Earth and the Sun. Its lit side faces away, and it is lost in the Sun’s glare.
  • At full Moon it is on the far side of the Earth from the Sun, and we see the whole lit face.
  • In between, we see part of the lit side: a crescent when less than half is lit, a quarter when exactly half of the disc is lit, and gibbous when more than half is.

The cycle from one new Moon to the next takes about 29.5 days. It is not the Earth’s shadow that makes the phases; that only happens during a lunar eclipse, at full Moon.

The eight phases

  1. New Moon
  2. Waxing crescent
  3. First quarter
  4. Waxing gibbous
  5. Full Moon
  6. Waning gibbous
  7. Last quarter (also called third quarter)
  8. Waning crescent

Waxing means the lit part is growing, waning that it is shrinking. A first quarter Moon looks like a half Moon: the name refers to a quarter of the way round its orbit, not to how much is lit.

When each phase is up

Because the phase depends on where the Moon is compared with the Sun, each phase keeps roughly the same hours:

Phase Rises around Best seen
Waxing crescent mid-morning low in the west after sunset
First quarter noon high at sunset, sets around midnight
Full Moon sunset all night, highest around midnight
Last quarter midnight high at sunrise, in the morning sky
Waning crescent before dawn low in the east before sunrise

The Moon rises on average about 50 minutes later each day, but this varies a lot with the time of year and your latitude.

Why the Moon tilts

The lit edge of the Moon always points towards the Sun. From Europe or North America, a waxing Moon in the evening is lit on its right and a waning Moon on its left. From Australia, New Zealand, South Africa or Argentina you see the Moon the other way up, so a waxing Moon is lit on its left, and the familiar face of the Man in the Moon is upside down.

Near the equator the evening crescent lies on its back like a bowl, because the Sun has set almost straight down below it. Even at one place, the tilt changes through the year and through the night as the Moon and the Sun move.

Earthshine

In the few days around new Moon you can often see the dark part of a crescent glowing faintly grey. That is earthshine: sunlight reflected from the Earth onto the Moon, and back to you. It is easiest to see in the evening twilight a few days after new Moon.
