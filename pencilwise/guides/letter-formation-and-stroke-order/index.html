Letter formation: why stroke order and direction matter

Correct letter formation means starting each letter in the right place, moving in the right direction and making the strokes in the right order. It matters because habits set early are hard to change, and letters formed the taught way become faster, more even and easier to join later, while reversals like b and d fade.

Updated 22 November 2026

What "formation" means

Teachers look at three things: where a letter starts, which way the pencil travels, and the order of the strokes. England's national curriculum puts it as forming "lower-case letters in the correct direction, starting and finishing in the right place". A letter can look right on the page and still be formed the wrong way, for example an o drawn clockwise from the bottom.

Why it matters

  • Speed and fluency. Letters formed with the taught movements flow into each other; letters drawn in odd ways slow writing down as children get older.
  • Joining later. Exit points and directions are chosen so that letters join smoothly. An a started at the bottom can't join to the next letter the way the class is taught.
  • Fewer reversals. Grouping letters by movement helps: b starts with a downstroke then a bump forward; d starts with the round shape going back. Children who learn the movement are less likely to swap them.
  • Habits stick. Motor habits formed at 4 or 5 are hard to change at 8. It is much easier to learn the movement right the first time.

Common patterns across school styles

  • Most letters start at the top. Round letters (c, a, d, o, g, q) start near the top right and go anticlockwise, "back" around.
  • Downstrokes go down: l, the stem of b, h, k and p.
  • Letters are taught in families that share a movement, which is why the order a school teaches letters often isn't alphabetical.
  • Short verbal pathways help: NSW teaches a as "around, up, down" and p as "down, up, around and back".

How to practise at home

  1. Watch it first. Show the letter being written stroke by stroke and say the pathway aloud.
  2. Trace it. Follow a model with a start dot and arrows, with a finger first, then a pencil.
  3. Write it alone. Fade the guide and let your child try, then compare with the model.
  4. Praise the start. Comment on starting in the right place and going the right way, more than on neatness.
  5. Little and often. Two or three letters a day, ideally from the same family.
