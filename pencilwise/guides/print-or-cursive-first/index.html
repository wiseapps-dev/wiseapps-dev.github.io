Print or cursive first? What schools actually do

Most English-speaking schools teach print letters first and join them later, often through a pre-cursive step with exit flicks. France teaches joined cursive lowercase from the start of formal writing, and many German schools now teach print with small exit curves where joining is optional. The right order is your child's school's order.

Updated 22 November 2026

Three common routes

Print first, then cursive. The usual route in the United States: children learn upright manuscript (often the ball-and-stick style, built from lines and circles) in kindergarten and first grade, and cursive later. Some schools use a slanted print with small tails, designed so that the move to cursive is shorter. California's AB 446 (2023) requires cursive or joined italics to be taught in grades 1 to 6, with the state's standards placing cursive in grades 3 and 4.

Print, pre-cursive, then joined. Common in England and Australia. Children learn print letters, then letters that end with an exit flick ready to join, then the joins themselves. England's national curriculum expects Year 2 pupils to "start using some of the diagonal and horizontal strokes needed to join letters", and Years 3 and 4 to use them. Australian states each have their own school style: New South Wales uses its Foundation Style.

Cursive from the start. In France, children read print but learn to write joined cursive lowercase in the first years of school, written in one continuous movement; cursive capitals come later, from CE1.

Germany sits between these: many schools teach the Grundschrift, print letters whose downstrokes end in a small exit curve. Children may join letters where it helps and leave them unjoined where it doesn't: "joining is possible, not required".

Is one order better?

Arguments go both ways. Print matches the letters children read, has fewer strokes to learn and is easier to read back. Cursive and joined writing can be faster and keeps letters together in words, and it avoids reversals such as b and d because each letter starts from the baseline. What matters most for a young child is consistency: one set of letter shapes, formed the same way every time, taught alongside the class.

What to do at home

  • Ask which style and which order the school uses, and copy it.
  • Practise formation, not neatness: the right starting point and direction matter more than perfect shapes at first.
  • If your child moves schools or countries, expect a short adjustment and be patient with mixed forms for a while.
