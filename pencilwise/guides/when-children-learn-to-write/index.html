When do children learn to write letters? Ages and stages

Most children learn to form letters at school between ages 4 and 6. They start with big movements and capital letters, learn lowercase letters on the lines in their first school year, and begin joining letters from about age 7, though the exact year and letter style depend on the country and the school.

Updated 22 November 2026

Before school: big movements first

Long before letters, children practise the movements letters are made of: lines down, lines across, circles and curves. Drawing, painting, playdough and tracing shapes in sand all count. Many children can write a few capital letters, often the ones in their own name, before they start school. Capitals are made of straight lines and simple curves, which is why they usually come first.

The first school year: letters on the line

In the first year of school, children are taught to form each letter correctly: where it starts, which way it goes and where it stops. England's national curriculum asks Year 1 pupils (age 5 to 6) to "sit correctly at a table, holding a pencil comfortably and correctly", to "begin to form lower-case letters in the correct direction, starting and finishing in the right place", to "form capital letters" and to "form digits 0-9", and to learn which letters belong to the same handwriting "families" (letters formed in similar ways).

In New South Wales, Australia, Kindergarten children learn the NSW Foundation Style: tall letters reach from the top dotted line to the bottom solid line, short letters stay between the solid lines, and long letters such as p reach down to the bottom dotted line. Each letter has a short verbal pathway, such as "around, up, down" for a.

Year 2 and after: size, then joining

Once letters are formed correctly, the next step is size and spacing. England's Year 2 pupils learn to "form lower-case letters of the correct size relative to one another" and to "start using some of the diagonal and horizontal strokes needed to join letters". Many schools prepare for this with pre-cursive letters that end in a small exit flick.

When joining starts varies widely:

  • United States: California's AB 446 (2023) requires cursive or joined italics to be taught somewhere in grades 1 to 6; the state's standards already include cursive in grades 3 and 4.
  • France: children learn joined cursive lowercase from the start of formal writing; cursive capitals come later, from CE1, and are often the last letters children write with ease.
  • Germany: many schools now teach the Grundschrift, print letters with small exit curves, and joining is optional; others still teach a joined script after print.

What this means at home

Follow the school. Ask the teacher which letter style the class uses (many schools send home a letter-formation sheet) and practise the same shapes, because a child who learns two ways of writing a letter has to unlearn one. Short, frequent practice works better than long sessions, and it should stay fun: a few letters a day, praised for starting in the right place, is plenty.

If writing is painful, very slow or a big struggle compared with classmates, mention it to the teacher; an occupational therapist can help with grip and coordination.
