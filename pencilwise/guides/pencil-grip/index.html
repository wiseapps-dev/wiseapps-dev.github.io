Pencil grip for young children: what to look for

Schools teach young writers a tripod grip: the pencil held between the thumb and index finger and resting on the middle finger, a little above the point, with a relaxed hand. Thicker or triangular pencils, a dot on the pencil where the fingers go and short, frequent practice help small hands settle into it.

Updated 22 November 2026

The tripod grip

The grip most schools teach is the tripod: the pencil is pinched between the thumb and index finger and rests on the side of the middle finger. The fingers sit a little way up from the point, not on the sharpened wood, and the other two fingers curl lightly into the palm. The wrist rests on the table and the pencil leans back towards the shoulder.

England's national curriculum asks Year 1 pupils to "sit correctly at a table, holding a pencil comfortably and correctly". The NSW Department of Education's guide for parents suggests a thicker pencil and the tripod grip, and a small dot or sticker on the pencil to show where the fingers go.

Signs a grip needs help

  • The pencil is held in a fist, or with all four fingers wrapped around it.
  • The thumb wraps over the index finger, so the fingertips can't move the pencil.
  • The child presses so hard the paper tears, or tires after a few letters.
  • The fingers sit right down on the point and block the view of the line.

Many young children change grip often at first; that's normal. Persistent pain, very slow writing or a lot of frustration are worth mentioning to the teacher. An occupational therapist can assess grip and hand strength.

Simple things that help

  • Short pencils and crayons. A short crayon leaves room for only two or three fingers, which nudges children towards a tripod.
  • Thicker or triangular pencils are easier for small hands to hold without squeezing.
  • Vertical surfaces. Drawing on an easel or paper taped to a wall builds the wrist position used for writing.
  • Finger strength through play: playdough, pegs, tongs, tearing paper and threading beads.
  • Short, frequent practice. Five minutes a day beats a long session.

Fingers on a screen

Tracing letters with a finger on a tablet practises something different from pencil grip: the movement of the letter, its starting point, direction and order of strokes. That movement pattern carries over to the pencil, so finger tracing and pencil practice work well together, as long as children also spend time holding a real pencil on paper.
