School handwriting styles by country, explained

Schools in different countries teach different letter shapes. US schools often start with ball-and-stick print, England and Australia with print that leads to pre-cursive and joined writing, Germany with print or the Grundschrift, and France with joined cursive. The differences show in letters such as a, k, y, t, 1 and 7.

Updated 23 November 2026

Why styles differ

Every school system picks a model alphabet so that a class learns one consistent set of shapes. The models differ in slant, in whether letters end with exit strokes, in the shape of a few letters, and in the order and direction of strokes. A child who moves between them notices most in a handful of letters.

United States

  • Ball-and-stick print. Upright letters made of straight lines and circles. Strokes start at the top; circles "go back" (anticlockwise). Capitals such as E and F start with the downstroke, then the slides across.
  • Slanted print with tails. Sloped print letters, many ending in a small tail (a, d, h, i, l, m, n, t, u), with a looped k and a fishhook y. Designed to make the step to cursive smaller.
  • Cursive. Joined letters with loops on b, e, f, h, k and l; taught in grades 3 and 4 in states such as California.

United Kingdom

English schools follow the national curriculum's expectations rather than one national alphabet. Print letters are taught in families: "long ladder" letters (l i t u j y), "one-armed robot" letters (r b n h m k p, hence a k with a loop), "curly caterpillar" letters (c a d o s g q e f) and "zig-zag" letters (z v w x). Many schools move to pre-cursive letters with exit flicks, then join.

Australia

Each state has its own style. New South Wales uses its Foundation Style: gently sloped print with simple verbal pathways such as "around, up, down" for a and "down, up, around and back" for p, before joined writing later; the ACT recommends the same style. Victoria uses Victorian Modern Cursive, also taught in Western Australia and the Northern Territory. Its beginners' letters differ in small ways, and in stroke order: the model's own arrows font starts M and N at the bottom and goes up, writes 0 clockwise from the top right, starts 7 at the top right going left, and makes x from a backwards c and a c. Queensland, South Australia and Tasmania have their own models: Queensland's b, d and q, for example, close their bowls with a diagonal into the stem, and Tasmania's J has no top bar.

New Zealand

New Zealand's school handwriting is a sloped style whose letters are written unjoined first and joined later; Playwrite NZ, an open font made in its style, draws both. The unjoined letters are close to Australia's in spirit, with their own shapes for letters such as k, t and y.

Germany, Austria and Switzerland

Children learn Druckschrift (print) first: single-storey a and g, a t with a small foot, and continental numbers, a 1 with an up-stroke and a crossed 7. Many schools now use the Grundschrift, print letters whose downstrokes end with a small exit curve (Wendebogen), where joining is optional. Others go on to a joined school script.

France

French children write joined cursive (écriture cursive) early, on Seyès ruled paper: loops on b, f, h, k and l, loops below the line on f, g, j, y and z, a distinctive r and s, and letters joined in one continuous movement. Cursive capitals come later. Capital letters in print (capitales d'imprimerie) are used for early writing.

Spain

Many Spanish schools teach letra ligada: joined lowercase letters with loops on b, f, h, k and l, an r and an s of their own, and every letter written from an entry stroke on the line, so that a word is written in one movement. Playwrite ES, an open font made in the style of the Spanish school model, pairs these with print capitals.

Elsewhere in Europe

Italy, the Netherlands, Poland, Portugal and Sweden have no single national print model; schools use common print forms with their own letters (Ą Ć Ę Ł Ń Ó Ś Ź Ż; Ç; Å Ä Ö) and local number forms, before the country's own joined script.

Which one to practise

The one your child's teacher uses. Pencilwise has styles for the US, the UK, NSW and the ACT, the German Grundschrift and French school cursive, drawn from stroke data that follows those models' published formation rules; styles for Victoria (with WA and the NT), Queensland, South Australia, Tasmania, New Zealand and Spanish school cursive, drawn from openly licensed fonts made in the style of those models (the AU School Handwriting Fonts and Playwrite), in each model's stroke order; and a common School print with the letters of German, French, Spanish, Italian, Dutch, Polish, Portuguese or Swedish. It doesn't have Italian, Polish, Portuguese or Dutch school cursive or the German joined scripts. No style is endorsed by a school authority.
