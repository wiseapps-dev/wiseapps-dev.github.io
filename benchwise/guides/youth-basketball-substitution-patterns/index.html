Basketball Substitution Patterns for Youth Teams

In a 40-minute youth basketball game, five players on court share 200 minutes. With 8 players and a change every 5 minutes, everyone plays 25 minutes; with 10, everyone plays 20. Subs go on at a stoppage, so treat each change time as a target and make it at the next dead ball.

Updated 2026-11-21. Written by the maker of Benchwise.

Basketball is the hardest junior sport to sub fairly by feel. Only five play at once, the game is fast, and you can't just wave a player on whenever you like. Without a plan, the confident kids stay on and the quiet ones sit.

This guide gives you the numbers for squads of 6 to 12, a few patterns that work, and the habits that make subbing at stoppages painless.

What the rules say about playing time

There's no single rule. It depends on your competition.

  • FIBA mini-basketball (for players 12 and under) has a long tradition of compulsory playing time. The FIBA mini-basketball rules, as published by Mini-Basketball England, split the game into four 10-minute periods. Each player must play in two periods (unless injured, disqualified or fouled out), and every player must sit out at least one full period. FIBA's coaching material for mini-basketball puts it more simply: all players must play in each half.
  • Some national leagues build it into the clock. Basketball England's "all must play" rule for some under 12 to under 16 competitions splits the first and third quarters into two 5-minute periods each, with the clock stopped at the halfway mark for changes. In a team of ten, every player must play one of those 5-minute periods in each of the two quarters.
  • Australian domestic competitions often play two halves of about 18 to 20 minutes with a running clock. Nunawading Basketball's junior domestic by-laws, for example, use 18-minute halves. The clock only stops for whistles in the last two minutes of the second half, and no subs are allowed in the final minute of the first half unless a player is injured or fouled out. Those by-laws don't set a minimum court time. That's left to clubs and coaches.

So check your association's by-laws, and your club's policy, before the season. If neither sets a rule, the rest of this guide is a fair default.

Minutes for squads of 6 to 12

Five spots for 40 minutes gives 200 player-minutes. If you sub about every 5 minutes, that's 8 stints and 40 stints of court time to share.

Squad Average Fair split (5-minute stints)
6 33.3 min 4 play 35, 2 play 30
7 28.6 min 5 play 30, 2 play 25
8 25 min everyone plays 25
9 22.2 min 4 play 25, 5 play 20
10 20 min everyone plays 20
11 18.2 min 7 play 20, 4 play 15
12 16.7 min 4 play 20, 8 play 15

Each split adds up to 40 stints. The same table works for two 20-minute halves with a running clock: it's still 8 stints of about 5 minutes.

Eight and ten are the easy numbers, because the minutes divide exactly. With any other squad size, some kids get one extra stint. Move that extra stint around from week to week.

Patterns that work

Ten players: two units, mixed each quarter

The classic is two units of five that swap every 5 minutes. It's simple, but the same five always play together, and one unit may be much stronger. A better version is to keep the 5-minute swaps but reshuffle the units at each break, so every kid plays with everyone across the game.

Eight players: three off, rolling

With eight, three sit each stint and everyone plays five of the eight stints. Stagger the changes so you're swapping two or three players at a time, not all five. Keep at least one confident ball handler on court through each change if you can.

Six or seven players: short rests

With a small squad, kids get tired before they get bored. Give each player one or two rests a half, and keep each rest to a single stint. A player who sits for 10 minutes straight in a game this short loses their rhythm.

Eleven or twelve players: plan it on paper

Big squads are where things go wrong. Twelve players means seven sit at any time, and each kid only gets three or four stints. Write the plan down before the game, and check that nobody sits for most of a half.

Subbing at stoppages

You can't make a change while the ball is live. Subs wait at the bench (or score bench) and come on when play stops and the officials let them. That has a few consequences.

  • Treat change times as targets. If the change is due at 5:00, send the subs to the bench at about 4:30 and make the change at the next dead ball. With a running clock, a stint might run 30 to 60 seconds long. That's fine.
  • Don't chase seconds. If a change runs a minute late, give the player who missed out a bit extra later in the game. Over a season, it evens out.
  • Watch the "no subs" windows. Some by-laws stop substitutions near the end of a half. Plan the last change of the half before that.
  • Use a helper. A parent at the bench with the plan, calling the next names, makes it much smoother.

Fouls, injuries and blowouts

  • Foul trouble: a player on four fouls may need to sit longer. Give them their minutes back later, or in the next game.
  • Injury: take the player off and share their remaining stints among the others. Don't rush them back to fit the plan.
  • Blowouts: if you're well ahead, rotate positions and let every kid bring the ball up. FIBA's mini-basketball advice even suggests subbing off a player once they've scored 10 points (they can come back on later).

Positions, not just minutes

Fair minutes include fair roles. At junior level:

  • Let every player bring the ball up the court.
  • Don't park your tallest kid under the basket all game. They need to learn to dribble and shoot from outside too.
  • Don't always put the same kids in the starting five. Starting matters to kids.

Talk to parents before the season

Tell parents your plan before the first game: everyone plays about the same, the times are targets because subs happen at stoppages, and the extra stint moves around each week. Then share the minutes after games. The talking to parents guide has wording you can copy.
