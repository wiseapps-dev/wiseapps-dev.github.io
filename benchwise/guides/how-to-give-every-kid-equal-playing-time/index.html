How to Give Every Kid Equal Playing Time

To give every kid equal playing time, multiply the spots on the field by the game length, divide by the players who turned up, then split the game into equal stints between changes. Share the stints so nobody is more than one apart, and give the extra stint to different kids each week.

Updated 2026-11-21. Written by the maker of Benchwise.

Most volunteer coaches agree with equal playing time. The hard part is doing it on a Saturday morning with nine kids, seven spots, a whistle that keeps going, and a parent asking if their child has had a go yet.

This guide gives you a method that works in any sport with rolling or timed changes. You count the minutes, split the game into equal stints, share the stints out, and keep a tally across the season. It takes ten minutes before the game, and it makes the sideline much calmer.

Why bother being exact

Kids don't sign up to sit on the bench. The Australian Sports Commission's Clearinghouse for Sport sums up the research on why children leave sport, and the theme is clear. Sport that feels too serious or too competitive drives kids away, especially the ones who are still developing. Kids who feel they can't take part equally tend to enjoy it less and drop out.

One study it cites asked young soccer players what makes the game fun. Trying your best, being treated with respect by the coach, and playing well as a team topped the list. "Competition" came in at number 21 and "winning" at number 48. Another example: in youth rugby, players who almost always got at least half a game reported more enjoyment and were more likely to keep playing.

Fair minutes also protect you. When you can show the numbers, conversations with parents are short and calm.

What the sports say (and where it varies)

Each sport and each club sets its own rules, so check yours. As a guide, here is what the national bodies publish at the time of writing:

  • Soccer (Football Australia): its game time policy template, which clubs can adapt, suggests equal game time for MiniRoos (under 6 to under 11). It also suggests rotating every player through every position, including goalkeeper, for example keeper in one half and outfield in the other. For under 12 to under 18, it suggests a minimum of half a game.
  • Australian rules football: the AFL's national junior rules handbook suggests rotating players at least every quarter, including through the interchange. It sets a minimum of 50 to 75% game time, with the exact figure left to each league.
  • Netball (NetSetGO): Netball Australia's modified rules fact sheet says game time should be spread evenly and players should try every position over the season.

Clubs and associations often go further, or have their own policies for older age groups. If your club has a written policy, that is the one to follow.

The method in five steps

1. Count the player-minutes

Multiply the number of spots on the field by the length of the game. That is the total playing time you have to share.

Example: soccer 7v7, two 20-minute halves. 7 spots × 40 minutes = 280 player-minutes.

2. Divide by the players who are here

280 ÷ 9 players = 31.1 minutes each. That's each player's fair share. You can't hand out 31.1 minutes exactly, which is why step 3 matters.

3. Split the game into equal stints

Choose how often you'll change. In soccer and hockey you can usually change any time. In basketball you change at stoppages. In netball, changes usually happen at the breaks. Split each period into equal stints that match.

With a change every 5 minutes, each 20-minute half has 4 stints, so the game has 8. Each stint has 7 spots, so there are 8 × 7 = 56 stints to share.

56 ÷ 9 = 6.2. So 2 players get 7 stints (35 minutes) and 7 players get 6 stints (30 minutes). Check it: 2 × 7 + 7 × 6 = 56.

That's what fair looks like in practice. Everyone's minutes are equal, or within one stint of each other. You can't do better without changing more often.

4. Lay it out on a grid

Draw a grid with players down the side and stints across the top. Fill it in so that:

  • each player gets their number of stints
  • nobody sits out two stints in a row if you can avoid it
  • everyone plays in both halves (or every quarter, where possible)
  • the keeper stays in goal for a whole half, and that time counts as playing time
  • changes are spread out, so you don't swap five kids at once.

5. Rotate the extra stint

When the minutes don't divide exactly, some kids get one more stint than others. Give that extra stint to different kids each week, and keep a simple season tally of minutes. Over a season it evens out, and you have the numbers to show it.

Positions count too

Equal minutes in the same spot every week isn't really equal. Kids get stuck as "the defender" or "the one in goal" quickly. A few habits help:

  • Rotate by thirds or zones. In soccer, footy and hockey, move each player between defence, midfield and attack across the game or across weeks.
  • Rotate goalkeeping. Unless a child really wants to keep goal every week and their family agrees, give everyone a turn. A half each is plenty.
  • Netball: keep a note of each player's positions so everyone gets a turn in the goal circle at both ends. The netball positions guide has a sample rotation.

Game day: what goes wrong and what to do

A kid arrives late. Add them in from the next change. They should get a fair share of the time that's left, but they aren't owed the minutes they missed. Some clubs prefer that late kids "catch up"; decide which way you'll do it and tell parents up front.

Someone gets hurt. Take them off and share their remaining stints among the others. Don't rush them back to keep the plan tidy.

A child doesn't want to go on. Let them sit for a stint, then offer again. Count it as a stint they were offered.

The change runs late. The ball doesn't go out, the referee doesn't stop play, or you lose track. It happens. Make the change at the next chance, and give the kid who missed out a little extra later.

One-sided games. If you're well ahead, use it: put kids in new positions and rest your strongest players. Netball Australia's modified rules suggest exactly this for lopsided junior games.

Common mistakes

  • Same starters every week. Starting matters to kids. Rotate it.
  • Garbage time. Five minutes at the end of a lost game isn't the same as five minutes in a close one. Spread each kid's stints through the game.
  • The long first-half sit. A child who sits for most of the first half often switches off. Try to get everyone on early.
  • Coach's kid. Your own child is the one other parents will watch. Treat them exactly like everyone else, and let the tally prove it.
  • Keeping it in your head. You'll forget. Write it down, or let an app do it.

Tell parents your approach

Tell parents what you're doing before the first game. A two-line message in the team chat is enough: you aim for equal minutes, keepers take turns, and late arrivals share the time that's left. Then share the minutes after each game. The guide to talking to parents about playing time has wording you can copy.
