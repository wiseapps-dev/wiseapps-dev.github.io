Talking to Parents About Playing Time

Most playing time arguments come from a gap between what the coach does and what parents expected. Write your approach down before the first game, share each player's minutes after every game and keep a season tally. When a parent raises it, talk away from the game: listen first, then show the numbers.

Updated 2026-11-21. Written by the maker of Benchwise.

Sooner or later, a parent will ask why their child sat out so long. Usually they're worried, not angry, and usually a two-minute conversation with the numbers in front of you settles it. The trick is to do most of the work before the season starts, so the question rarely comes up.

This guide covers what to say before the first game, how to share minutes, and how to handle the conversation when it happens. There are messages you can copy along the way.

Before the season: say it once, clearly

Most playing time arguments come from a gap between what the coach does and what the parent expected. Close that gap early.

Researchers in sports coaching and family psychology from CQUniversity and the University of Queensland suggest a frank chat with parents at the start of the season. Some of them also coach junior teams. One exercise they describe: ask each parent to share out 100 points between winning, skill development and personal development, then compare. It's a quick way to find out what your parents actually care about, and it makes your approach easier to explain.

Write your approach down and share it. Keep it short:

Our playing time approach this season Everyone gets about equal minutes. When the minutes don't divide exactly, some kids get one extra stint, and that moves around each week. Keepers take turns, a half each. Kids who arrive late join at the next change and share the time that's left. I'll share the minutes after each game. If you have a question, message me and we'll find a time to chat, but not straight after a game.

If your club already has a policy, use its wording and link to it. Football Australia's game time policy template, for example, says equal time for under 6 to under 11, with every player rotated through every position, including goalkeeper. From under 12, it suggests a minimum of half a game, with more earned through effort and attitude.

Give parents a job

The same CQUniversity and University of Queensland researchers suggest giving parents practical roles: setting up, fruit at half-time, and even running the substitutions. A parent holding the rotation sheet and calling out the next change sees exactly how fair it is. They also stop worrying about it.

Show the numbers

Fair is easier to believe when you can see it.

  • Share minutes after each game. A short list in the team chat is enough: name and minutes.
  • Keep a season tally. One bad week (a late arrival, an injury, a change that ran late) looks very different next to the season average.
  • Be honest about the odd stint. If a child got 25 minutes and others got 30, say so, and say they'll get the extra stint next time.

Minutes v Hawks, Saturday Ava 30, Isla 30, Kai 25, Leo 25, Mia 30, Noah 25, Ollie 30, Ruby 30, Sam 30, Zara 25. Next week Kai, Leo, Noah and Zara get the extra stint. Season averages are all within 2 minutes of each other.

When a parent raises it

Even with all of that, someone will ask. Here's how to keep it calm.

Not straight after the game

Emotions run high at the ground. Youth coaches quoted by the Positive Coaching Alliance say it's never a good idea to raise playing time right after a game or in the heat of the moment. One suggests waiting 24 hours, because most of what felt urgent sorts itself out by then. Put that in your pre-season message, then stick to it kindly.

Thanks for raising it. I want to give it proper attention. Can I call you Tuesday evening?

Listen first

Let them say what they saw. Don't argue with what it felt like from the sideline, even if your sheet says otherwise. Often the worry is about something else: the child is upset, or was left out of a position they love.

Then show the numbers

Open the minutes for that game and the season. Most of the time, this is where it ends: "Oh, I didn't realise she'd played the whole first half."

Explain, then agree a next step

If the numbers show your child got less, say why: arrived late, came off injured, a change ran late. Then say what you'll do: "She'll start next week and get the extra stint." If you made a mistake, own it. Coaches who say "I got that wrong, here's how I'll fix it" rarely hear about it again.

Let the player ask

For older kids, the Positive Coaching Alliance suggests the player, not the parent, should talk to the coach. A good question for a child to ask is, "What do I need to work on?" A good coach answers with specific things they can practise. Help them prepare, and be ready to give them a real answer.

When minutes aren't equal on purpose

There are fair reasons a child might play less, such as an injury, or a code of conduct matter your club handles. Football Australia's template lists injury, poor attitude and breaking the code of conduct as exceptions, and says time lost that way isn't made up later. If you use exceptions like these:

  • tell parents about them in your pre-season message
  • tell the parent directly when it happens, not through the team chat
  • keep it rare and keep it consistent.

If it doesn't settle

Sometimes you won't agree. That's what the club is for. Football Victoria's MiniRoos guidelines, for example, say parents who think their child isn't getting fair time should raise it with the club's MiniRoos coordinator. Other sports and clubs have their own junior coordinators. Point the parent there. It's not a failure; it's how the club is meant to work.

A quick checklist

  • Pre-season message sent, with your playing time approach and how to raise concerns
  • Club policy linked, if there is one
  • A parent helper for subs or the clock
  • Minutes shared after every game
  • Season tally kept
  • Concerns handled away from the ground, after a day
