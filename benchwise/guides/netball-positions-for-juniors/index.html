Netball Positions for Juniors and How to Rotate Them

Netball has seven positions, GS, GA, WA, C, WD, GD and GK, and each is limited to certain thirds of the court. Most junior coaches change at the quarter breaks, so time comes in whole quarters. With 9 players, one plays all four quarters and eight play three, and everyone tries every position over the season.

Updated 2026-11-21. Written by the maker of Benchwise.

Netball gives every player a bib and a patch of court. That's great for teaching, but it also means a junior can spend a whole season at wing defence without ever touching the ball in the goal circle. A simple rotation fixes that, and it's easier to plan than it looks.

This guide covers the seven positions, the NetSetGO rules that shape junior games in Australia, when you're allowed to make changes, and a sample rotation for a squad of nine.

The seven positions

The court is split into thirds: a goal third at each end and a centre third. Each end has a goal circle. Every position has areas it's allowed in.

Position Bib Where they can go Main job
Goal shooter GS Attacking third, including the goal circle Shoot goals, hold space in the circle
Goal attack GA Centre third and attacking third, including the goal circle Feed and shoot, link with the centre
Wing attack WA Centre third and attacking third, not the goal circle Take the centre pass, feed the shooters
Centre C Everywhere except the two goal circles Take the centre pass, link attack and defence
Wing defence WD Centre third and defensive third, not the goal circle Mark the wing attack, win the ball back
Goal defence GD Centre third and defensive third, including the goal circle Mark the goal attack, defend the circle
Goal keeper GK Defensive third, including the goal circle Mark the goal shooter, rebound

Kids often see it as attack (GS, GA), mid-court (WA, C, WD) and defence (GD, GK). That grouping is handy for rotating.

NetSetGO: the junior pathway

NetSetGO is Netball Australia's program for younger children. Its FAQ describes three stages:

  • Net (ages 5 to 6): about 45 minutes of fun activities, with lower rings and soft balls. No formal match play.
  • Set (ages 7 to 8): about 60 minutes, split between skills and modified match play.
  • Go (ages 9 to 10): up to 60 minutes of modified match play and activities.

The national modified rules fact sheet sets the match rules for the older stages. Western Australia's NetSetGO umpire handbook uses the same numbers and names them Set and Go:

Set (7 to 8) Go (9 to 10)
Game length 4 × 8-minute quarters 4 × 10-minute quarters
Ball Size 4 Size 4
Goal post height 2.4 m 2.4 m to 3.05 m
Time to pass Up to 5 seconds Up to 4 seconds
Defence Strict one-on-one, no defending the shot One-on-one, may defend the shot
Scores Not kept May be kept, but no ladder

Three rules matter most for rotations:

  1. Unlimited substitutions at any time. The player coming on tags the player coming off.
  2. Game time should be spread evenly among all players.
  3. Players should try all positions over the program or season.

The fact sheet also suggests that in one-sided games, coaches and umpires should do what they can to keep it a good experience for everyone. That can mean putting players in unfamiliar positions or resting the stronger players.

Your association may run NetSetGO slightly differently, so check its local rules.

After NetSetGO: when can you change?

Once kids move into regular junior competition, most associations play the Rules of Netball, sometimes with local changes. Netball Australia's 2026 fact sheet on tactical changes covers two kinds of change: a substitution (a bench player comes on) and a team change (two on-court players swap positions). These are allowed:

  • at the intervals: quarter time, half time and three-quarter time
  • straight after a goal is scored, if a player asks the umpire to hold time
  • when play is stopped for injury, illness or blood.

There's no fixed limit on how many changes you make, but an umpire can step in if changes become disruptive.

In practice, most junior coaches plan their changes for the quarter breaks. It's calm, everyone can hear you, and each player's time is a whole quarter. That's the approach in this guide. Some competitions, including NetSetGO, allow rolling substitutions, so check yours.

How the minutes work with quarters

With changes only at the breaks, you have 4 quarters × 7 positions = 28 quarters of court time to share.

Squad Quarters each In minutes (4 × 10)
7 Everyone plays all 4 40 each
8 4 play all 4, 4 play 3 40 or 30
9 1 plays all 4, 8 play 3 40 or 30
10 8 play 3, 2 play 2 30 or 20
11 6 play 3, 5 play 2 30 or 20
12 4 play 3, 8 play 2 30 or 20

Each row adds up to 28. Whoever gets the extra quarter this week should sit a little more next week. A season tally keeps that honest.

A sample rotation for 9 players

Here's a four-quarter plan for a squad of nine (players A to I). Everyone sits once, except A, who plays all four. A different player gets that full game each week. No one plays the same position twice, and everyone gets time in both attack and defence.

Player Q1 Q2 Q3 Q4
A GS C GK WD
B off GS WD C
C off GA C GK
D GA off GS GD
E WA off GD GS
F C WD off GA
G WD GK off WA
H GD WA GA off
I GK GD WA off

How it was built:

  1. Decide who sits each quarter. Two per quarter with nine players. Spread them out so nobody sits twice.
  2. Fill the positions one quarter at a time. Check each player's earlier quarters and avoid a repeat.
  3. Balance ends of the court. If a player was at GS or GA, try them at GD, GK or WD next.
  4. Check the circle. Every player should get a turn in a shooting position (GS or GA) across a few weeks.

Tips that make it easier

  • Write it on a card. At quarter time, read out the next quarter's bibs. The kids will learn to look for their name.
  • Rotate the bench too. Don't always rest the same kids in the same quarter. Sitting out the last quarter every week feels different from sitting out the first.
  • Use a season tally. Mark each player's positions after every game. By mid-season you'll see who has never played centre or keeper.
  • Expect preferences. Some kids only want to shoot, and some are nervous in the circle. Give everyone a go, and rotate more slowly for the nervous ones rather than skipping them.
  • Don't box in tall kids. Height helps at GS and GK, but tall kids need to learn the whole court too.
