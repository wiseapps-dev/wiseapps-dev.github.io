How to memorise a monologue

Updated 21 November 2026, by the maker of Scenewise

To memorise a monologue, read it aloud until you understand every line, then map it as a chain of thoughts with a short label for each. Learn it a section at a time, always running from the top, use first letters to get it word-perfect, then put it on its feet and test it under pressure before the audition.

A monologue is harder to learn than a scene in one important way: nobody hands you your cues. In a scene, the other actor's last line triggers your next one. In a monologue, the only cue for each thought is the thought before it. If one link in the chain goes, there's nothing outside you to pull you back on track.

So the job is to build that chain deliberately. Here's a step-by-step way to do it, with an example from Shakespeare.

Step 1: Choose one you can connect with

The easiest monologue to learn is one you understand and care about. Acting coach Jami Tennille, writing for Backstage, makes the point that relating to a speech's intentions, emotions and conflict makes the words far easier to retain. If you're choosing a piece, read widely and pick one that grabs you.

Check the rules before you fall in love with a speech. Drama schools are specific. NIDA's acting application, for example, asks for two contrasting monologues of about two minutes each, filmed as one continuous video. One is contemporary (from roughly the early 1900s to now) and the other uses heightened language or verse. They need to come from published dramatic texts, and NIDA suggests a list to choose from. Requirements change from year to year, so read the current guide for wherever you're applying.

Step 2: Read it whole, aloud, until you understand every line

Read the full play if you can, so you know who your character is talking to, what has just happened, and what they want. Then read the monologue aloud several times.

For older or heightened text, Trinity College London suggests translating it into your own words first. Paraphrase every line in plain modern English. If you can't paraphrase a line, you don't understand it yet, and a line you don't understand is very hard to remember.

Step 3: Map the thoughts

Now break the speech into thoughts. A new thought usually starts with a new image, a new argument or a change of tactic. Give each thought a two- to four-word label and write the labels down in order. This list is the skeleton of the speech.

Here are the opening lines of Jaques's speech from As You Like It:

All the world's a stage, And all the men and women merely players; They have their exits and their entrances, And one man in his time plays many parts, His acts being seven ages.

The skeleton might be:

  1. World = stage, people = players
  2. Exits and entrances
  3. One man, many parts
  4. Seven ages (and then the speech walks through them in order: infant, schoolboy, lover and so on)

That last step is a gift. The speech has a built-in order, so once you know the seven ages you know the shape of the rest. Many speeches have structure like this: an argument with points, a story with events, a list that builds. Find it and use it.

Psychologists who study actors describe this kind of work as deep, meaningful processing. The MIT Press Reader's summary of the research makes the point that attending to meaning, rather than surface features of the words, is what produces lasting memories.

Step 4: Learn it section by section, then chain

Don't try to learn the whole speech at once. Tennille recommends learning it in segments, with breaks between sessions. Trinity suggests just one to three lines at a time.

A reliable pattern is cumulative:

  1. Learn thought 1 until you can say it without looking.
  2. Learn thought 2. Then say 1 and 2 together.
  3. Learn thought 3. Then say 1, 2 and 3.
  4. Carry on, always running from the top.

The joins between thoughts are where monologues break, so practise them specifically: say the last line of one thought and the first line of the next, over and over. Some actors also learn backwards, starting with the last section, so the ending, where nerves often bite, is the best-rehearsed part. Trinity lists building backwards as one of its techniques.

Step 5: Use first letters to get it word-perfect

Once you know the shape, you need the exact words. Both Tennille and Trinity recommend the first-letter technique: write the first letter of each word and try to recall the full line from those letters. The opening of the Jaques speech becomes:

A t w a s, A a t m a w m p;

The letters stop you guessing at the general idea and make you produce the actual words. Use the free first-letter tool to make the letter version, and read the first-letter method guide for how to fade the letters out.

Step 6: Use the verse if there is any

If your speech is in verse, the rhythm is a memory aid. Shakespeare's iambic pentameter has a regular da-DUM beat, ten syllables to a line. Trinity points out that when a line feels a beat short, you've probably dropped a word. The line endings matter too: learn where each verse line breaks, not just the sentences.

Step 7: Put it on its feet

Sitting still and reciting is the least useful way to rehearse a monologue. Stand up and move. Research on actors found lines learnt with movement were remembered better, and Trinity suggests linking lines to specific moves, like crossing on a particular phrase.

Most of all, say it to someone. Pick an imaginary listener, place them in the room, and try to change their mind, make them laugh or make them understand. A speech said for a reason is easier to remember than a speech recited.

Tennille's last tip is simple: keep the text in front of you. Print it out and stick it where you'll see it, and keep a copy on your phone for the bus.

Step 8: Stress-test it

An audition room will make you nervous, and nerves attack memory. Make the speech robust before you get there:

  • Start from any thought on your skeleton, not just the top.
  • Say it fast, as a speed-run.
  • Say it while doing something else.
  • Say it to a friend, or record a take on your phone and watch it back.

If a particular join keeps failing, go back to Step 4 and drill that join on its own.

A one-week plan

Day Focus
1 Read the play. Read the speech aloud. Paraphrase it.
2 Map the thoughts. Learn the first third.
3 Second third, then run from the top.
4 Final third, then run from the top. First letters for the tricky lines.
5 Run blank. Drill the joins. Put it on its feet.
6 Stress-test: random starts, speed-runs, a recorded take.
7 One or two calm runs. Rest.
