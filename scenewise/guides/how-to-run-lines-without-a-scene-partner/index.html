How to run lines without a scene partner

Updated 21 November 2026, by the maker of Scenewise

To run lines without a scene partner, record the other parts with gaps for yours, or cover your line with a card and check it after each attempt. Read one side at a time, move while you run, brief a friend who isn't an actor, or use a line-reading app that reads the cues aloud and stops for you.

The best way to run lines is with another person reading the other parts. They give you your cues from outside your own head, at a pace you don't control, which is what happens on stage or on set. But scene partners have jobs, families and lives, and it's 11 pm.

Here are six ways to run lines on your own, what each is good for, and the mistakes that make solo practice less useful than it could be.

Why running alone is harder

When you read a scene to yourself, you already know what's coming. You see your line on the page before you've "heard" the cue, so you never practise the real skill: hearing the end of someone else's line and having yours arrive. Every method below is a way of getting the cue outside you and your line hidden.

1. Record the other parts, leave gaps for yours

This is the most popular solo method among working actors. In a Backstage reader survey, one actor described recording the other characters' lines in a neutral tone, with silences where his own lines go. Another records cues and lines scene by scene and plays them on repeat, trying to get each line out before the recording does.

To do it well:

  • Record each scene as a separate file, named by scene.
  • Read the other parts plainly. If you perform them with big choices, you'll learn to react to your reading, and be thrown when the real actor does something different.
  • Leave a gap a bit longer than your line takes to say. Too short and you'll rush, too long and the rhythm dies.
  • Make a second version with your own lines included, for listening on the train before you can say them yourself.

Casting Networks suggests the same thing, and adds a refinement: listen specifically for the last word before each of your lines, and write those cue words on cards.

2. The cover card

Old-fashioned and very effective. Take a piece of card and slide it down the page so that the cue line is visible and your line is covered. Say your line out loud. Slide the card down to check. If you missed anything, say the line correctly three times before moving on.

For a version you can carry around, use index cards: write the cue on one side and your line on the other. One actor in the Backstage survey uses exactly this for Shakespeare.

3. Read one side at a time

Another actor in the same survey reads only his own lines through the scene, then only the other characters' lines, then reads the scene cold. Reading just the other parts shows you where the gaps for your lines fall. It's a good way to notice which of your lines you can't yet place: if you get to a gap and have no idea what goes there, that's the line to work on.

4. Move while you run

Don't sit still. Studies of actors by psychologists Helga and Tony Noice, summarised by the Association for Psychological Science, found that actors remembered lines learnt while making a matching movement, like walking across the stage, better than lines learnt without one. The movement didn't even need to be repeated when they were tested.

So run your lines while you walk, cook or clean. One actor in the Backstage survey does exactly that: she reads a line, says it aloud while moving, and repeats. If you know your blocking, mark it out in the lounge room and walk it.

5. Rope in a non-actor, and brief them

A housemate, partner or parent can read the other parts even if they've never acted. Casting Networks notes that a non-actor giving a straight read works fine for running lines.

It helps to tell them what a good reader does. Casting director Joseph Pearlman, writing for Backstage about self-tape readers, describes the ideal as engaged but restrained. Specifically:

  • Not robotic or monotone. A flat read leaves you nothing to react to.
  • Not whispering. Normal speaking volume.
  • No character voices or accents. They're distracting.
  • Not competing. The reader is there to support you, not to perform.

Ask them to tell you when you get a word wrong, not just when you dry. Otherwise paraphrases sneak in and set.

6. Use a line-reading app

Text-to-speech can play the other parts for you. You paste or import the script, choose your character, and the device reads every other line, stopping for yours. It's the recording method without the recording.

Be realistic about what you get. The voices are synthetic: clear, endlessly patient, and flat. They're great for learning the words and the cue pattern. Mistakes that waste solo practice

Running lines silently in your head. Casting Networks lists this as a common mistake. Silent recall feels faster but doesn't build the memory you'll use out loud. Speak at performance volume at least some of the time.

Learning only your own lines. The same article recommends learning the whole scene. Other characters' lines carry your cues, and knowing them helps you recover if someone skips or paraphrases.

Locking in one reading. If you always say a line the same way, at the same speed, you'll struggle when the director asks for something different. Casting Networks suggests varying it once you know the words: faster, slower, a different emphasis.

Never testing without help. Recordings and cards are support. At some point you have to run the scene with nothing to look at.

A 15-minute solo session

  1. 3 minutes: read the scene aloud, all parts, to remind yourself what's happening.
  2. 5 minutes: cover card, line by line, cue visible.
  3. 5 minutes: run it with a recording or app, on your feet.
  4. 2 minutes: list the lines you dropped. Start there next time.
