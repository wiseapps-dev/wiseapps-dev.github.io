The first-letter method for memorising text

Updated 21 November 2026, by the maker of Scenewise

The first-letter method is one of the simplest ways to learn text word for word. You write out only the first letter of each word, keeping the punctuation, and then try to say the full text using just those letters as prompts. It's recommended by acting teachers, and it's built into the free first-letter tool on this site.

Here's what it is, why it works, and how to use it so it helps rather than becoming a crutch.

What it looks like

Take the opening line of Twelfth Night:

If music be the food of love, play on;

Reduced to first letters, it becomes:

I m b t f o l, p o;

Every word gets a letter, in order. Punctuation and line breaks stay exactly where they were, because they carry the rhythm and the breath. Trinity College London uses this same line as its example of the technique, and Backstage's monologue tips describe it too.

Here are the first lines of Jaques's speech from As You Like It:

A t w a s, A a t m a w m p; T h t e a t e,

If you know the speech, you can read that out loud almost as easily as the original. If you half know it, you'll feel each word click into place, or not. That feeling is the point.

It's not an acronym

People sometimes confuse this with acronym mnemonics, like using a made-up word to remember a list. That's a different tool for a different job. An acronym compresses a list into one memorable word. The first-letter method keeps every word of a passage, in order, as a prompt. It's for text you need word-perfect: lines, speeches, poems, vows, readings.

Why it works

It makes you retrieve, not reread

The most reliable finding in memory research is that recalling something strengthens memory more than looking at it again. In a well-known 2006 study, Roediger and Karpicke had students either reread prose passages or try to recall them. After five minutes, rereading looked better. After two days and after a week, the students who had practised recalling remembered substantially more, even though the rereaders felt more confident.

Reading a full script is rereading. Reading first letters isn't: the letter m doesn't tell you "music", you have to pull "music" out of memory. You get the benefit of testing yourself with enough support to keep going.

It's a "desirable difficulty"

Elizabeth and Robert Bjork use the phrase desirable difficulties for practice conditions that feel harder in the moment but produce better long-term learning. They also stress the word desirable: a difficulty only helps if you can overcome it. If the task is too hard, you just flounder.

That's why first letters are so useful as a middle step. Going from the full text straight to a blank page is often too hard too soon. You guess, get it wrong and lose confidence. First letters are hard enough to force recall and easy enough that you succeed most of the time.

It keeps you honest about the exact words

When you run lines from memory, it's easy to say the gist and not notice. The letters catch you. If you say "if music is the food of love", the letter b is sitting there telling you the word was "be".

How to use it, step by step

  1. Understand the text first. Read it aloud a few times and make sure you know what every line means. Trinity's advice is to work out the meaning and intention before anything else. Letters can't help you remember words you never understood.
  2. Make the letter version. Trinity suggests writing it by hand, which adds some effort that helps. If time is short, the first-letter tool makes it instantly and lets you copy it into your notes.
  3. Say it aloud from the letters. Out loud, at a normal pace, not mumbling in your head.
  4. Check against the original. Mark every word you got wrong or had to look up.
  5. Drill the marked lines. Say each one correctly three times, from the letters.
  6. Fade the letters out. Once a section is solid on letters, try it blank. Go back to letters only for the parts that fall apart.

The full ladder

Blank Nothing The real test

Tips

  • Keep the punctuation. Commas and full stops tell you where to breathe and how the thought is shaped.
  • Keep the line breaks for verse. Verse lines are units of sense and rhythm.
  • Watch for runs of the same letter. "T t t" might be "to the town" or "take the train". If a phrase keeps tripping you, reveal it and drill it in full.
  • Don't stay on letters forever. They're scaffolding. If you only ever practise from letters, you'll learn to need them. Always finish a session with at least one blank run.

Using it for scripts

For a scene with other characters, convert only your own lines and leave the cue lines in full. You want to practise the real moment: read the cue, then produce your line from its letters. For a fuller method, see how to memorise lines fast and how to memorise a monologue.
