How to self-tape without a reader

Updated 21 November 2026, by the maker of Scenewise

To self-tape without a reader, ask any friend to read, even a non-actor, in the room or over a video call. Otherwise book a paid reader, or play the other lines from a recording or an app, though casting prefers a live voice. Whoever reads stays off camera, next to the lens and quieter than you.

The sides arrive, the tape is due tomorrow, and nobody is around to read the other part. It's one of the most common self-tape problems, and casting knows it. Here are the four realistic ways to get the other lines read, when each one fits, and how to set up the tape so the reader helps you rather than distracts.

First, the reassurance

You don't need an actor, and you don't need to spend money. Australia's self-tape guidelines, prepared by members of MEAA/Equity in consultation with agents and casting directors, accept that a non-actor can read for you. They also say that if you're struggling to find a reader, you can tell your agent and the casting director, and they'll try to help.

The UK's code of best practice, from the Casting Directors' Guild together with Equity and the agents' associations, says a reader often helps but shouldn't be an absolute requirement, and that actors aren't expected to pay for one. It counts three kinds of reader: in person, virtual, or a pre-recorded app.

What casting wants from any reader

Whichever option you choose, the reader's job is the same. Casting director Caroline Liem's self-tape rules, published by Backstage, and SAG-AFTRA's guide to self-tapes agree on the basics:

  • Off camera, beside the lens. Liem puts the reader right next to the camera, about a step away, at your eye level. That keeps your eyeline close to the lens, which Spotlight recommends so casting can see both sides of your face.
  • Quieter than you. Standing by the camera puts the reader close to the microphone, so Liem asks them to speak more softly than you. SAG-AFTRA's guide adds that you should be the one closest to the recording device, with the reader further back if needed. Casting is there to hear you.
  • Engaged, not performing. Casting director Alexis Koczara, talking to Casting Networks, wants the reader to add to the scene "but not be overpowering". No accents, no big character choices.
  • Keep going after a slip. A fluffed word from the reader doesn't ruin a take. The same article suggests asking them to carry on rather than stopping.

These rules apply just as much to a voice coming out of a laptop or a phone speaker.

Option 1: a friend in the room

Best for: almost every tape, if anyone at all is free.

A live person is what casting prefers. Koczara puts it plainly: "It's always better to use a live person." A partner, housemate or parent who has never acted is fine. Brief them before you start:

  • Send them the sides ahead of time, so they know the scene well enough to look up at you.
  • Read plainly, at a natural pace, a little quieter than you.
  • Sit or stand right beside the camera, with their eyes at the same height as yours.
  • Don't act, don't do voices, and don't stop if they stumble.

If you have actor friends, offer a swap: you read for their tapes, they read for yours. Casting Networks suggests an actor friend as the best choice, because a trained reader gives you more to play against.

Option 2: a friend on a video call

Best for: a reader who's free but somewhere else: across town, interstate or in another time zone.

The UK code counts a virtual reader as a reader. The set-up is what makes it work:

  1. Use two devices. Record on your phone and take the call on a laptop, tablet or second phone.
  2. Put the call beside the lens. Prop the call device right next to your camera, at eye level, so you naturally look near the lens when you listen.
  3. Use the speaker, not earphones. Earphones show on camera, and the tape needs to pick up the reader's lines, just more quietly than yours.
  4. Turn the call volume down. The speaker sits close to your phone's microphone, so it can easily drown you out. Record a short test, play it back, and adjust until the reader sits under you.
  5. Silence both devices. A notification sound in the middle of a take means starting again. SAG-AFTRA lists ringtones among the background noises to watch for.
  6. Allow for lag. Video calls often run slightly behind. Overlapping lines and rapid back-and-forth suffer most, so ask your reader to come in cleanly on your cue rather than talking over you.

Option 3: a paid reader

Best for: a role you really want, comedy or fast dialogue where timing matters, or a run of auditions with nobody to ask.

Self-tape studios provide a reader along with the camera, lights and sound, and there are online services that connect you with a reader over video for a session fee. A practised reader gives you real timing and something to react to.

Two honest caveats. First, it isn't required. In the UK, actors aren't expected to pay for readers, and in Australia the guidelines' answer to "I can't find a reader" is to talk to your agent and the casting director. Second, check what you're paying for: whether the reader sees the sides beforehand, how long the session runs and how many takes fit into it.

Option 4: record the reader, or use an app

Best for: the night before a morning deadline, or when nobody is free at all.

Be clear-eyed about this one. Koczara told Casting Networks she doesn't like actors reading against recordings of themselves, because the tape becomes about fitting lines between the recorded ones rather than about the performance. The UK code does list a pre-recorded app as an acceptable kind of reader, so it's allowed. But it's the fallback, not the first choice.

If you go this way, make it sound as live as you can:

  • Use someone else's voice if you can. A friend who can't be there when you tape can often record the lines earlier, from anywhere. Reading against your own recorded voice is exactly what Koczara objects to.
  • Record plainly and quietly. Neutral, unhurried, no performance, at a lower level than you'll speak.
  • Avoid fixed gaps. One long recording with silences built in sets your timing for you: pause for a thought and the next line arrives anyway. An app that waits for you to finish lets you play the scene at your own pace.
  • Play it from beside the lens, as you would with a live reader, and do a test take to check the volume.

Setting up the tape

The reader is only part of it. These apply however the other lines get read:

  • Read the instructions first. Spotlight tells you to read any instructions you've been sent thoroughly. The casting brief overrides everything here, from framing to slates.
  • Film in landscape. SAG-AFTRA and Spotlight both say to record horizontally unless you're told otherwise.
  • Camera at eye level, framed close. Spotlight suggests a mid-close shot, from the chest to the top of your head. The Australian guidelines limit what casting can ask for to mid-shots or close-ups, and say a tape filmed on a smartphone is quite enough.
  • Eyeline just beside the lens. Play the scene to your reader, next to the camera. Don't look into the lens unless you're asked to.
  • Slate only if asked, and as asked. Liem's advice is to slate when it's requested. SAG-AFTRA's guide suggests recording the slate as a separate clip, with at least your name and a full-length shot. Briefs vary, so follow yours.
  • Keep the sides off camera. SAG-AFTRA suggests taping them up near you so you can steal a glance. Put them by the lens, next to your reader, and a glance won't pull your eyeline away.
  • A few takes, not twenty. Spotlight's rule of thumb: if it isn't working within three takes, take a break and come back to it. Send only the takes you're asked for.
