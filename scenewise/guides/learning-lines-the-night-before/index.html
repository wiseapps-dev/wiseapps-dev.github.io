Learning lines the night before an audition or self-tape

Updated 21 November 2026, by the maker of Scenewise

With sides the night before, aim to be off-book enough rather than word-perfect. Understand the scene first. Learn the first and last lines until they're automatic, then the turning points. Run the rest out loud against your cues, go to bed, because sleep helps lock it in, and run it twice from memory in the morning.

The email arrives at 6 pm: sides attached, audition tomorrow at 11, or a self-tape due in 36 hours. It happens all the time, and there's a sensible way to use the hours you have. Here's how off book you really need to be, what to learn first, a plan for the evening, and why going to bed is part of the plan.

First, what are sides?

Sides are the pages you're given to audition with: a scene or a few scenes from the script, sometimes only a few lines, sometimes several pages. Spotlight's guide to sides notes that they're sometimes "dummy sides", written specially for the audition when the real script is confidential. Either way, they're all you've got, so read them closely.

How off book do you need to be?

Less than you fear, usually. Casting Networks describes what most casting directors want as "off-book enough": you know the material well enough to be free in it, not necessarily word-perfect. Brad Holbrook, writing for Backstage about self-tapes, goes further: casting won't hold it against you for using the sides when you've had little notice, because what they want to see is that you understand the character. He warns that straining to look off book can kill the performance, and that it's better to glance at the page naturally.

Spotlight adds two cautions. Expectations vary by audition and casting director, so if you're not sure, ask your agent. And if you're likely to lose the words under pressure, holding the sides is the safer choice.

So the goal for tonight is: know the scene, own the first and last lines, and be familiar enough with the rest to look up from the page.

Understand before you memorise

With little time, it's tempting to start drilling words straight away. Spotlight's advice for short-notice preparation is the opposite: spend your time on the character and the circumstances, because that's what will most help the performance. Holbrook agrees: explore the scene, the character and what they want, and you'll naturally rely on the page less.

Ask the basic questions. Who am I talking to? What just happened? What do I want from them? What's stopping me? If you have the full script, or can find a synopsis, read it.

What to learn first

Holbrook's tip for cold material is to memorise the first and last lines so you start strong and finish strong. The middle can lean on the page if it has to. The top and tail are where you need to be looking at your reader, or the lens.

After that, learn the turning points: the moment your character changes tactic, gets bad news or makes a decision. Those are the moments casting will watch most closely.

A three-hour plan for the evening

Time What to do
30 min Read the sides three times, aloud. Answer who, what, where, why. Read the full script if you have it.
20 min Mark the beats. Write a few words in the margin for what you want in each one.
20 min Learn the first and last lines until they're automatic.
50 min Learn the rest in small chunks against the cues: cover your line, say it, check. Use first letters for the tricky ones.
30 min On your feet. Run it with someone, a recording of the other lines, or a line-runner. Record one test take on your phone and watch it back.
10 min One last calm run. Then stop.

Casting Networks suggests picking one main technique when time is short, rather than flitting between methods. Their go-to is writing the lines out and recording the other parts, starting the night before. Whatever you choose, make sure it involves saying the lines out loud: silent reading in your head doesn't prepare you for speaking them.

Then go to bed

This isn't just about being fresh. Sleep helps lock in what you learnt.

Scientific American reported a study by Stéphanie Mazza and colleagues at the University of Lyon. Forty French speakers learnt 16 Swahili–French word pairs, then relearnt them 12 hours later. One group learnt in the morning and relearnt that evening. The other learnt in the evening, slept, and relearnt the next morning. The group that slept needed roughly half as many attempts to relearn the list, about three instead of six. A week later they remembered about 15 pairs against 11, and six months later about 9 against 3.

Notice the structure: learn, sleep, relearn. In the same study, a group that slept but didn't relearn did no better. So the night-before plan has a second half:

In the morning

  1. Run the scene twice from memory, before you look at the page. Note what's slipped.
  2. Fix those lines, with their cues.
  3. Run it once more on your feet.
  4. On the way to an in-person audition, Casting Networks suggests listening to a recording of your lines.

Then put it away. Over-drilling on the day tends to make a performance rigid, and the room may well ask you to try it a different way.

Self-tape notes

  • Place the sides where you can see them without dropping your eyes. Casting Networks suggests keeping them in line with your reader, so a glance down doesn't break your eyeline.
  • Brief your reader. Plain, engaged reading at normal volume. See how to run lines without a scene partner for what makes a good reader. No one to read at all? How to self-tape without a reader covers video-call readers, paid readers and recorded lines.
  • Use the sides honestly. Holbrook suggests that if the character might plausibly hold paper, like a doctor with a chart or a lawyer with notes, you can work the pages into the scene.
  • Do a few takes, then choose. The first take is often stiff and the fifth is often stale. Somewhere in the middle is your audition.

Don't lock in one reading

Spotlight makes the point that a panel is looking for flexibility and how you take direction, not just a polished take. If you've drilled the lines in exactly one rhythm, a redirection can knock the words out. Once you know the scene, run it once very differently: faster, quieter, angrier. If the words survive that, they'll survive the room.
