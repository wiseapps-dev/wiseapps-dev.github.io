How to learn lines for a play: a rehearsal timeline

Updated 21 November 2026, by the maker of Scenewise

Learn lines for a play in stages that follow rehearsals. Read the play and mark your part before the first rehearsal, learn each scene soon after it's blocked, and learn your cue lines along with your own. Be off book by the date the director sets, then use line notes to fix the words you keep getting wrong.

Learning lines for a play isn't one big task. It's a series of smaller ones spread across rehearsals, and the timing matters as much as the method. Learn too early and you lock in choices before you've explored the scene. Learn too late and you're still holding the script when everyone else is acting.

This guide walks through a typical rehearsal period, what you should know by each stage, and how to handle the off-book date, cue lines and line notes.

How a rehearsal period usually runs

Every company works differently, but most productions follow roughly this shape:

  1. Read-through. The cast reads the play aloud together.
  2. Blocking. The director sets where everyone moves, scene by scene. You have your script in hand.
  3. Off-book date. After this date, you can't hold a script in rehearsal. If you forget, you call "Line" and the stage manager, who is "on book" following the script, reads it to you.
  4. Working rehearsals and runs. Scenes are refined, then the play is run in full.
  5. Tech and dress. Lights, sound, costumes and set come in. Long days, little time for actors' work.
  6. Opening.

The off-book date is the one that matters for this guide. A stage management guide from Santa Clara University puts it simply: there's usually a date after which actors can no longer carry scripts, and from then on they call for lines.

A sample timeline

Here's how that might look for a six-week amateur or community production. Professional rehearsal periods are often shorter and more intense, so compress accordingly.

When Rehearsals Your line work
Before week 1 — Read the whole play twice. Mark your lines and cues.
Weeks 1–2 Read-through, blocking Learn each scene soon after it's blocked.
Week 3 Blocking finishes Run every scene you're in, with cues.
Week 4 Off-book No script. Call "Line" when stuck.
Week 5 Runs Fix line notes. Daily speed-runs.
Week 6 Tech, dress, open Keep the lines warm. Don't learn anything new.

The safest personal deadline is a week before the official off-book date. That way the off-book rehearsal is a test you're ready for, not the first time you've tried.

Before the first rehearsal: read, then mark up

Read the whole play at least twice before you start learning anything. You need to know the story, your character's arc and how each scene connects.

Then mark up your script. Doug Atkins, talking to Backstage about memorising a one-person play, describes highlighting each character's lines in a different colour and flagging sections with sticky notes so he can find any scene fast. For a play with several actors, a simple version is:

  • One highlighter colour for your lines.
  • A second colour, or underlining, for your cue lines: the last few words of the line before each of yours.
  • A tab on the first page of each of your scenes.

Weeks 1–2: learn scene by scene, right after blocking

Atkins describes the rule from his training: block the scene with the script in hand, then be off book the next time you run that scene. It's a good rhythm. Once a scene is blocked, the moves give you physical cues for the words, and the words give you the reasons for the moves. Atkins says blocking is his main memory cue.

Learn in small sections. Read a beat aloud, cover it, say it from memory, check, repeat. For the method itself, see how to memorise lines fast.

Learn your cue lines as well as your lines

A cue is whatever makes you speak. Usually it's the end of another character's line, but it can also be a move, a sound effect or a lighting change. Atkins mentions music and lighting cues that he worked out with the creative team and that trigger his next lines.

You need to know your cues well enough to come in on time without thinking. That means rehearsing with them, not just running your own lines in order. Read or listen to the cue, then say your line. If you have no one to read with you, see how to run lines without a scene partner.

A common problem is the "lost cue": the other actor paraphrases or skips a line, and you don't hear your cue. Knowing the whole scene, not just your bits, lets you recover. You know what's being talked about, so you know what you say next.

Off-book day: calling for a line

You will forget something. Everyone does. When you do:

  1. Stay in the scene, in position and in character.
  2. Say "Line" clearly, without apology.
  3. Take the line from the stage manager, and say it.
  4. Carry on.

Don't break character to explain, and don't paraphrase your way out of it. A paraphrase hides the gap from you and confuses your scene partner, who is waiting for a particular cue.

Line notes: the most useful feedback you'll get

Once you're off book, many stage managers give line notes: a list of every place you departed from the script. A stage management guide published by Texas's University Interscholastic League uses a key for the common types:

  • Dropped words – you left something out.
  • Skipped line – a whole line missing.
  • Called line – you asked for it.
  • Changed words – a different word in place of the scripted one.
  • Inverted words – right words, wrong order.
  • Added words – extras that aren't in the script.
  • Paraphrasing – the gist, not the text.
  • Missed cue – you came in late or not at all.

Theatre teacher Craig Mason, writing for Theatrefolk, gives good advice on receiving them: don't argue or explain, circle the note in your script, and say thanks. A line note isn't a judgement of your acting. It's a technical correction. And small words matter: "a" instead of "the" can change what a line means.

Fix your notes the same day. Drill only the flagged lines, with their cues, until they're right three times in a row.

Keeping lines once you have them

Lines fade if you stop running them, especially during tech week, when you spend hours standing around waiting while the lights get focused.

  • Do a quick speed-run of your scenes each day: say everything at top speed, no acting, just words.
  • Run your scenes from random starting points.
  • Before each run or performance, run the first line of each of your scenes.

Big parts

A lead may have a third of the play or more. Start earlier: try to know your first act before blocking reaches the second. Ask the director if any scenes are being blocked first, and learn those first. And protect your sleep: it's when your brain consolidates what you learnt that day.
