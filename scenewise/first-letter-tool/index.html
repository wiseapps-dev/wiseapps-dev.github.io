How to use it

  1. Paste your text and read it aloud on Full two or three times, so you know what it means before you test yourself.
  2. Switch to Some. About a third of the words become gaps. Say the whole thing aloud, filling them in. Tap a gap only when you're truly stuck.
  3. Move to Most, then Letters. With only first letters left, each letter is a nudge, not an answer: you still have to pull the word out of memory.
  4. Finish on Blank. You see the shape of the text and nothing else. If you can get through it, you know it.
  5. Copy first letters and paste them into your notes. It's a pocket cue sheet for the train or the wings.

"Some" and "Most" hide different words each time you tap New gaps, and "Most" always includes the words "Some" hid. Words in brackets, like stage directions, are never hidden.

Why first letters work

Rereading feels productive, but memory research keeps finding that pulling something out of your head does more for long-term recall than looking at it again. First letters make you do the pulling, with just enough help to keep going. The first-letter method guide explains the evidence and how to use it for scripts, speeches and poems.

Learning a part, not just a speech?

This tool is for a single block of text. For a scene, you also need your cues: the lines just before yours. Run lines online reads the other parts aloud and hides your lines at the level you choose.
