Cost per wear explained

Cost per wear is what a piece of clothing costs each time you wear it: the price divided by the number of wears. A $120 pair of sneakers worn 192 times costs about 63 cents a wear; a $60 dress worn twice costs $30 a wear. The lower the number, the harder a piece works.

Updated 21 November 2026

The formula

Cost per wear = price ÷ number of wears.

That's all. A $90 pair of jeans worn 90 times costs $1 a wear. Worn 9 times, it costs $10 a wear. The price is fixed the day you buy something; the number of wears is the part you control.

You can try your own numbers in the cost per wear calculator.

Counting wears honestly

Count days, not outfits. If you wear a jacket to work and then out to dinner, that is one wear. Closetwise counts it the same way: one wear per piece per day.

For things you owned before you started counting, make an estimate of how often you've worn them. Closetwise calls this "worn before" and adds it to the wears you log, so a coat you've had for six winters doesn't look like it has never been worn.

Why cheap can cost more

A $25 tee you wear 40 times costs 63 cents a wear. A $15 top worn twice before it loses its shape costs $7.50 a wear, twelve times as much. Cost per wear doesn't say "spend more"; it says "spend on what you'll wear".

It also works the other way. A pricey piece you seldom reach for can quietly be your most expensive item per wear, and the number shows it.

Using it before you buy

Estimate honestly: how many days a month will you wear it, and for how many months? Multiply, then divide the price by the result. If you can't name three things it goes with, lower the estimate. If it replaces something you wear constantly, raise it.

A useful check is the number of wears it needs to reach a target, such as $1 a wear. A $150 coat needs 150 wears. Is that three winters, or ten?

What the number leaves out

Cost per wear ignores cleaning, repairs and anything you might get back by selling or giving a piece away. It also ignores joy: a dress worn to one perfect wedding may be worth every cent. Use the number to spot patterns, not to judge single pieces.

Wearing clothes longer matters beyond money

The longer you wear a piece, the lower its cost per wear, and the lower its footprint too. Writing for OpenLearn, WRAP's Sarah Clayton notes that extending the wearable life of a garment by just three months would cut the carbon, water and waste it accounts for by 5 to 10%. WRAP's research also put the clothes in the average UK household at around £4,000, so wearing them more is a large saving hiding in plain sight.

To find the pieces that aren't earning their place, try a not-worn-in-90-days list.
