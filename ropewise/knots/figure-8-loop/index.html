How to tie a figure-eight loop

Climbing knots. These knots are used in climbing and other activities where a mistake can kill. Ropewise shows how each knot is tied. It doesn't teach climbing and doesn't replace instruction. Learn them from a qualified instructor, and have your knots checked, before you rely on one. Follow your equipment maker's instructions.

Life-safety knot. Dress all four strands flat and check it before every use. Leave a long tail, at least 10 cm (about a hand-width). Some instructors teach a backup knot: follow your instructor. For climbing, learn it from a qualified instructor and have it checked.

Not a substitute for instruction. Not for life-safety use without training.

Figure-eight loop. A fixed loop tied in a doubled rope, easy to check because it shows two parallel figure eights. It is widely used in climbing for a loop at the end of a rope that clips to a carabiner. It is hard to untie after a heavy load.

Origin and references

Origin
A traditional loop with no documented inventor, also called the Flemish loop. Ashley lists it as #1047; it is the figure-eight knot tied in a doubled rope.

Every knot weakens the rope it is tied in, and any knot can slip or jam. Check it each time you tie it.

Common mistakes

  • Leaving crossed strands. Dress all four strands flat and parallel before tightening.
  • Pulling only the loop. Tighten each of the four strands in turn.

See also

  1. Alpine butterfly: A fixed loop tied in the middle of a rope. It takes load on the loop or along the rope in either direction.
  2. Double fisherman's: Joins two ropes, or the ends of one cord into a loop, with a double overhand from each end round the other.
  3. Figure-eight follow-through: A figure eight tied in the rope, threaded through the harness and retraced to form the tie-in loop.
