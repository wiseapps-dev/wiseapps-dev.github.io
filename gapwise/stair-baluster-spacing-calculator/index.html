The sum for one tread

Balusters on a stair usually stand two or three to a tread, evenly spaced so the pattern repeats up the flight. With n balusters on each tread, the centres are the going divided by n, and the clear gap is the centres minus the baluster width:

centres = going ÷ n
gap     = going ÷ n − baluster width
largest going for n = n × (limit + baluster width)

The gap between upright balusters is measured level, so the slope of the stair doesn't change it. The going is the level distance from one nosing to the next, not the depth of the tread board, which overhangs the riser below.

Worked examples

  • US, 10 inch treads, 1 1/4 inch balusters: two per tread give 10 ÷ 2 − 1 1/4 = 3 3/4 inches, under 4 3/8. Two per tread works for a going under 11 1/4 inches; deeper than that, use three.
  • England, 250 mm going, 41 mm spindles: two per tread give 125 − 41 = 84 mm, under 100 mm. Two work for a going under about 282 mm.
  • Australia, 250 mm going, 42 mm balusters: one per tread leaves 208 mm, far over 125 mm. Two give 83 mm.

Questions

How many balusters per stair tread?

Usually two or three. Divide the going by the number per tread and take off the baluster width: that's the gap, and it must be under your limit. Deeper treads and thinner balusters need three.

Which width do I use for turned spindles?

The narrowest part. A turned or tapered baluster leaves its widest gap where it is thinnest, and that is the gap the sphere test catches.

Do I measure along the handrail?

No. The gap between upright balusters is measured level, so use the going, the level distance from nosing to nosing.
