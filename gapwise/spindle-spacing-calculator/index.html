Spindle spacing calculator (UK)

Under Approved Document K, guarding in a home that under-fives may use must not let a 100 mm sphere through, so every gap between spindles must be under 100 mm. For the count, take the length between newel posts minus 100, divide by the spindle width plus 100, and round up. If it's a whole number, add one.

The UK rule in one line

Approved Document K (England) says guarding in a building that children under five may use must not let a 100 mm sphere through any opening, and shouldn't be easy to climb (paragraphs 1.39 and 3.3). A toddler could live in or visit any home, so plan on 100 mm. That's why the trade talks about a 99 mm gap: every gap has to be under 100 mm, not on it. The calculator's UK preset keeps every gap under the limit.

The rest of the rules for homes

  • Guarding is needed where there's a drop of more than 600 mm.
  • Height: at least 900 mm on stairs, landings and the edges of internal floors, and 1100 mm on external balconies (Diagram 3.1).
  • Handrails on stairs sit 900 mm to 1000 mm above the pitch line.
  • Open risers must stop a 100 mm sphere too (paragraph 1.9).

This is a summary for England, not legal advice. Wales publishes its own Part K guidance, Scotland's domestic Technical Handbook also uses a 100 mm sphere (clause 4.4.2), and Northern Ireland has its own technical booklets. The balustrade gap rules guide compares the UK with Australia, New Zealand and the US. Check with your building control body. Gapwise doesn't certify compliance.

Worked example: a 2.4 m landing

The newel posts are 2400 mm apart and the spindles are 41 mm square. With spindles between two newels there is a gap at both ends, so n spindles make n + 1 gaps.

count   = (2400 − 100) ÷ (41 + 100) = 16.3, round up to 17
gap     = (2400 − 17 × 41) ÷ 18     = 94.6 mm
centres = 41 + 94.6                 = 135.6 mm

Check one fewer: 16 spindles give (2400 − 656) ÷ 17 = 102.6 mm, which is over the limit. With 32 mm spindles the same landing needs 18 at 96.0 mm. Mark each spindle from the newel face, not by stepping the gap along, so small errors don't add up.

How many spindles per tread?

On the stair itself, spindles usually sit two to a tread. With two per tread the gap is about half the going minus the spindle width:

  • 41 mm spindles: a 250 mm going gives 125 − 41 = 84 mm. Two per tread works up to a going of about 280 mm.
  • 32 mm spindles: a 250 mm going gives 93 mm, but from a 264 mm going the gap reaches 100 mm. Use three per tread, or thicker spindles.

To space spindles along a whole flight, measure the level distance between the newels, not the length along the handrail. The gap between upright spindles is measured level, so the slope doesn't change it. To set out the stair first, use the stair calculator with the UK preset.

Fewest spindles, or a closer look?

Stair part suppliers often plan on gaps of about 80 mm, which uses more spindles and leaves room for error. The calculator finds the fewest spindles that keep every gap under 100 mm. For a closer look, type a smaller largest gap and it works out the count for that instead.

Questions

What is the maximum gap between spindles in the UK?

Under 100 mm. A 100 mm sphere must not pass through any opening in the guarding, which is why people call it the 99 mm rule. A gap of exactly 100 mm fails.

How many spindles do I need for a 2.4 m landing?

With 41 mm spindles, 17 at a 94.6 mm gap. With 32 mm spindles, 18 at 96.0 mm. For other sizes, type them into the calculator.

Do I measure along the handrail on the stairs?

No. Use the level distance between the newel posts, because the gap between upright spindles is measured level. Or work it out per tread: half the going minus the spindle width, with two spindles a tread.

Does the calculator make my staircase compliant?

No app can do that. The preset keeps every gap under 100 mm, but heights, handrails and climbability have rules too, and they vary across the UK. Check with your building control body.
