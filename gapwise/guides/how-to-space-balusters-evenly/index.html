How to space balusters evenly

Updated 2026-11-20 · Written by a tradie · Calculator: Baluster spacing calculator

To space balusters evenly, count them first: the length between posts minus the maximum gap, divided by the baluster width plus that gap, rounded up. The exact gap is the length minus all the baluster widths, divided by the count plus one. Then mark every baluster from the first post, never by stepping the gap along.

Spacing balusters by eye rarely works. You end up with a fat gap at one post, or a last gap over the limit.

Here's the baluster spacing formula, a metric example, an inch example, and why you never step a gap along the rail. Spindle spacing works the same way.

What you need

  • L: the clear distance between the posts, inside face to inside face.
  • w: the width of one baluster, across the face you see along the rail.
  • The maximum gap: 125 mm in Australia, 100 mm in New Zealand houses and UK buildings that under-fives may use, and 4 inches under the US residential code. Balustrade gap rules explains each.

The baluster spacing formula

Balusters between posts have a gap at both ends, so there's always one more gap than balusters.

  1. Count: n = (L − max gap) ÷ (w + max gap), rounded up to the next whole number.
  2. Exact gap: g = (L − n × w) ÷ (n + 1).
  3. Marks: the left edge of baluster i sits at i × g + (i − 1) × w from the first post.

If the gap comes out exactly on the limit, add one more baluster. The rules say a sphere must not pass, so exactly 125 mm isn't good enough.

Drilling for round spindles? Mark centres instead: centre i = i × g + (i − ½) × w.

Step by step on site

  1. Measure between the posts at the top rail and the bottom rail. Posts are rarely dead plumb. Use the larger number: if its gaps pass, the narrow end passes too.
  2. Measure a few balusters and use the typical width. For turned spindles, use the thinnest part between the rails, because that's where the gap is widest.
  3. Work out the count, then the exact gap.
  4. Work out every mark from the first post, not from the last mark.
  5. Hook the tape on the first post once and mark every baluster from there.
  6. Before fixing, check the gap to the far post. It should match within a millimetre.

Worked example 1: 2400 mm between posts, 42 mm balusters, under 125 mm

  • Count: (2400 − 125) ÷ (42 + 125) = 2275 ÷ 167 = 13.62. Round up to 14.
  • Gap: (2400 − 14 × 42) ÷ (14 + 1) = (2400 − 588) ÷ 15 = 1812 ÷ 15 = 120.8 mm.
  • Check the total: 14 × 42 + 15 × 120.8 = 588 + 1812 = 2400 mm. ✓
  • Check one fewer: 13 balusters gives (2400 − 546) ÷ 14 = 132.4 mm. That's over 125, so 14 is the minimum.

Centre to centre is 42 + 120.8 = 162.8 mm. Marks to the left edge of each baluster, from the first post:

Worked example 2: 72 inches between posts, 1 1/2 inch balusters, under 4 inches

  • Count: (72 − 4) ÷ (1.5 + 4) = 68 ÷ 5.5 = 12.36. Round up to 13.
  • Gap: (72 − 13 × 1.5) ÷ 14 = (72 − 19.5) ÷ 14 = 52.5 ÷ 14 = 3.75 in (3 3/4").
  • Check the total: 13 × 1.5 + 14 × 3.75 = 19.5 + 52.5 = 72 in. ✓
  • Check one fewer: 12 balusters gives 54 ÷ 13 = 4.15 in. Over 4, so 13 it is.

Centres are 1 1/2 + 3 3/4 = 5 1/4". Marks to the left edge of each baluster: 3 3/4, 9, 14 1/4, 19 1/2, 24 3/4, 30, 35 1/4, 40 1/2, 45 3/4, 51, 56 1/4, 61 1/2 and 66 3/4". The last one finishes at 68 1/4", leaving 3 3/4" to the post. ✓

Why you mark from the start instead of stepping along

The tempting method is to mark the first gap, then step 162.8 mm from each mark to the next. Every step carries a small error: play in the tape hook, the pencil line, rounding 162.8 to 163. Stepping adds those errors together.

Take example 1. Say each of your 14 measurements runs 1 mm short. The last baluster lands 14 mm early, and the final gap is 120.8 + 14 = 134.8 mm. That's over the limit, and you only find out at the far post. Run 1 mm long each time and the last gap shrinks to 106.8 mm.

Rounding alone does it too. Mark the first gap at 121, step 163 thirteen times, and the last baluster finishes at 121 + 13 × 163 + 42 = 2282 mm. The last gap is 118 mm, not 120.8.

Marking from the first post means each mark carries only its own error. A spacer block is handy while fixing, but check it against your marks.

Allow for timber movement

Timber Queensland's data sheet on moisture puts average shrinkage across the growth rings, from green to 12% moisture content, at under 3.5% to over 8%, depending on species. Kiln-dried balusters move far less, but they still move with the weather.

When balusters shrink, every gap grows by about what each baluster loses. A 42 mm green hardwood baluster shrinking 5% loses 42 × 0.05 = 2.1 mm, so a 120.8 mm gap opens to about 122.9 mm. That's still under 125, but it's tight.

If your timber isn't seasoned, or you want margin, work to a smaller limit. Use 120 mm in example 1: (2400 − 120) ÷ (42 + 120) = 2280 ÷ 162 = 14.07, which rounds up to 15 balusters. The gap is (2400 − 15 × 42) ÷ 16 = 1770 ÷ 16 = 110.6 mm. One extra baluster buys you about 14 mm of headroom.

FAQ

How many balusters do I need for 2.4 m? With 42 mm balusters under the 125 mm rule, you need 14 (example 1). Under a 100 mm rule it's 17. For other sizes, run the formula.

Is baluster spacing measured as the gap or as centres? The rules limit the clear gap. Centres (gap plus baluster width) are just handy for marking out.

Does the formula work on stairs? Yes, using the horizontal distance between the posts, because the gap between vertical balusters is measured horizontally. To mark along the sloping rail instead, stretch every mark by rail length ÷ horizontal length. Stairs have extra rules, covered in balustrade gap rules.

My panels are different lengths. Should they all use the same gap? Work out each panel separately. Gaps a few millimetres different between panels go unnoticed. A fat gap at one post doesn't.

What if I'm doing pickets, not balusters? Pickets across the rails usually run edge to edge, which needs a different formula: see how to space fence pickets evenly. To cut balusters from stock lengths, see how to plan a cut list.
