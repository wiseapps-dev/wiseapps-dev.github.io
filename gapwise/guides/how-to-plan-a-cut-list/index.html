How to plan a cut list with the least waste

Updated 2026-11-20 · Written by a tradie · In the app: Gapwise cut list

To plan a cut list with the least waste, list the pieces longest first and add one saw kerf to every piece and to each stock length. Fill each length with the longest pieces that still fit, use offcuts before buying more, and finish with the shortest stock length that takes what's left.

A linear cut list turns "I need these pieces" into "buy these lengths and cut them like this". Done well, it can save a length or two, and it stops the classic mistake: three 600s that won't come out of a 1.8 m length.

Here's how to do it by hand, with kerf, the longest-first method, mixed stock lengths and offcuts.

What you need

  1. The pieces: each length and how many, longest first.
  2. The stock lengths you can buy. Australian timber mostly comes in 300 mm steps. One Australian supplier lists pine framing from 2.4 m to 6.0 m in 300 mm increments, but H3 treated pine from 3.6 m to 6.0 m in 600 mm steps. Check what your yard has in your size.
  3. Your saw's kerf: the width the blade turns into sawdust. Mafell lists its 185 mm, 24-tooth circular saw blade at 2.4 mm. Full-kerf blades are typically 1/8" and thin-kerf blades 3/32". Not sure? Allow 3 mm (1/8").
  4. The ends: if they're split, rough or out of square, plan to trim them.

The cuts rule: n pieces take n cuts, or n − 1

Every cut eats one kerf. Cut n pieces from a length with an offcut left and that's n cuts. If the pieces use the length exactly, the last piece is just what's left, so it's n − 1 cuts.

The easy check by hand: add one kerf to every piece, and one kerf to the stock length. If the padded pieces fit the padded stock, the cuts work.

With a 3 mm kerf:

  • Three 600s from 1.8 m: 3 × 603 = 1809, against 1803. No. The third piece comes out 1800 − 603 − 603 = 594 mm.
  • Two 2700s from 5.4 m: 2 × 2703 = 5406, against 5403. No.
  • One 2700 from 2.7 m: 2703 against 2703. Yes, with no cut, if the ends are square.

The longest-first method

  1. Sort the pieces from longest to shortest.
  2. Start a new length with the longest piece left.
  3. Fill the rest with the longest piece that still fits, padded. Repeat until nothing fits.
  4. Write down the offcut and start the next length.
  5. For the last few pieces, choose the shortest stock length that takes them.
  6. Use any offcut that's long enough for a piece before buying more.

Longest first isn't always the best possible answer, but it's usually close, and it fits on the back of a docket. Cut long pieces first on site too, so a mis-cut can still become a short piece.

Worked example 1: a landing frame (metric, 3 mm kerf)

You need 2 × 2700, 5 × 1500 and 6 × 450 mm: 15,600 mm of pieces. Padded, they're 2703, 1503 and 453.

Using 6.0 m lengths (6003 padded):

  • Length 1: two 2700s take 5406, leaving 597. A 1500 won't fit; a 450 (453) will, leaving 144. Cut 2700, 2700, 450. Offcut: 6000 − 5850 − 3 cuts × 3 = 141 mm.
  • Length 2: four 1500s need 4 × 1503 = 6012, too long. Three take 4509, leaving 1494, and three 450s take 1359. Cut 1500 × 3, 450 × 3. Offcut: 6000 − 5850 − 6 × 3 = 132 mm.
  • What's left: 1500, 1500, 450, 450 (3900 mm). A 3.9 m length won't take them: 3912 padded against 3903. A 4.2 m will, leaving 4200 − 3900 − 4 × 3 = 288 mm.

Buy 2 × 6.0 m + 1 × 4.2 m = 16.2 m. The waste is 16,200 − 15,600 = 600 mm (3.7%). Check: offcuts of 141 + 132 + 288 = 561 mm, plus 13 cuts × 3 mm = 39 mm, makes 600. ✓

Mixing stock lengths. Buy the two 2700s as 2.7 m lengths, which need no cut if the ends are clean. Then:

  • 6.0 m: 1500 × 3, 450 × 3 as before. Offcut 132 mm.
  • What's left: 1500, 1500, 450, 450, 450 (4350 mm, 4365 padded). A 4.5 m length (4503) takes it, leaving 4500 − 4350 − 5 × 3 = 135 mm.

Buy 2 × 2.7 m + 1 × 6.0 m + 1 × 4.5 m = 15.9 m. The waste is 300 mm (1.9%). Check: 132 + 135 = 267 mm of offcuts, plus 11 cuts × 3 mm = 33 mm, makes 300. ✓

That's 300 mm less timber. Check the price per metre, though: some lengths cost more per metre, so the least waste isn't always the cheapest.

Using an offcut. Say there's a 1.6 m offcut in the ute. A 1500 comes out of it (1503 is under 1603), leaving 97 mm. Back on 6.0 m lengths, lengths 1 and 2 are as before, leaving 1500, 450 and 450 (2400 mm). A 2.4 m length won't take them (2409 padded against 2403); a 2.7 m will, leaving 2700 − 2400 − 9 = 291 mm.

Buy 2 × 6.0 m + 1 × 2.7 m = 14.7 m. That's 1.5 m less than the first plan.

Worked example 2: a shelving frame (imperial, 1/8" kerf)

You need 3 × 45", 4 × 32" and 2 × 18" (299" of pieces) from 8 ft (96") or 10 ft (120") lengths. Padded, the pieces are 45 1/8, 32 1/8 and 18 1/8, and the stock is 96 1/8 and 120 1/8.

  • Length 1 (8 ft): 45 + 45 take 90 1/4 padded, leaving 5 7/8. Nothing else fits. Offcut: 96 − 90 − 2 × 1/8 = 5 3/4".
  • Length 2 (8 ft): 45 + 32 + 18 take 95 3/8 padded. Offcut: 96 − 95 − 3 × 1/8 = 5/8".
  • What's left: 32, 32, 32, 18. Three 32s need 96 3/8 padded, against 96 1/8 for an 8 ft length, so the third would come out 31 3/4". A 10 ft length takes all four (114 1/2 padded), leaving 120 − 114 − 4 × 1/8 = 5 1/2".

Buy 2 × 8 ft + 1 × 10 ft = 312" (26 ft). The waste is 312 − 299 = 13". Check: offcuts of 5 3/4 + 5/8 + 5 1/2 = 11 7/8", plus 9 cuts × 1/8" = 1 1/8", makes 13". ✓

With 8 ft lengths only, the third length takes 32 + 32 + 18 and the last 32 needs a fourth length. That's 384", or 72" more timber.

FAQ

How much kerf should I allow? Measure a scrap board, cut 100 mm off one end, then measure what's left. Whatever is missing beyond the 100 mm is your kerf. If you can't measure it, allow 3 mm or 1/8".

Why did my last piece come out short? Kerf. Two or more pieces that add up exactly to the stock length never fit, because the cuts between them take timber too. Use the padding check before you buy.

What does a cut list optimiser do that I can't? A cut list optimiser (or optimizer), or a timber cut list calculator, does the same job as longest-first but tries far more combinations, including mixed stock lengths and offcuts on hand. On a big job it can find savings you'd miss on paper.

Can I use this for balusters and pickets? Yes. Work out the count first, with how to space balusters evenly or how to space fence pickets evenly, then cut them from stock using this method. For the gap limits balusters must meet, see balustrade gap rules. For deck boards, the free decking calculator works out the lengths to buy with the saw kerf allowed for.
